Front Matter: Author’s Note, Sources, and Timeline

Author’s Note

Why This Book

India’s prime ministers are usually remembered through elections, ideology, personality, and domestic transformation. Nehru is invoked for the republic’s founding imagination. Shastri for resolve and simplicity. Indira Gandhi for Bangladesh and the Emergency. Vajpayee for Pokhran and Lahore. Manmohan Singh for economic reform and restraint. Narendra Modi for assertion and scale.

Yet there is another way to read them: through the moments when the nation stood at the edge of force.

This book began from a simple question. What happens to a prime minister when the border catches fire? Not only what policy is chosen, but what kind of India is revealed in the choosing. A war, a terrorist attack, a treaty, a nuclear test, a refugee crisis, a diplomatic rupture — each of these events presses upon the state until the inner shape of power becomes visible. Speeches are tested by logistics. Ideology is tested by intelligence reports. National pride is tested by casualty lists. Strategic autonomy is tested by fuel, finance, aircraft, sanctions, and votes in Parliament.

The story of India’s wars and geopolitical crises is not a separate military chronicle standing outside politics. It is one of the central narratives of the republic. Partition and Kashmir shaped the first years of freedom. China in 1962 altered India’s strategic consciousness. The 1965 war restored military confidence after humiliation. The 1971 war remade the subcontinent. Pokhran-I and Pokhran-II declared India’s refusal to live permanently inside a nuclear hierarchy designed by others. Siachen turned cartographic ambiguity into a glacial deployment that has lasted for generations. Sri Lanka taught the limits of regional intervention. Kargil proved that nuclear weapons did not abolish limited war. Mumbai 26/11 forced India to decide between retaliation and restraint under the eyes of a furious public. Galwan reminded the country that agreements on paper require power on rock.

The gap this book seeks to fill lies between two existing traditions. One tradition writes India’s prime ministers as political biographies. Another writes India’s wars as military histories. Both are necessary. But the prime ministerial war room is where personality, party, Parliament, intelligence, diplomacy, military capacity, economic strength, public emotion, and historical memory meet. That is the room this book tries to enter.

It does so without a partisan thesis. India’s prime ministers have belonged to different parties, traditions, regions, generations, and temperaments. Some were poets, some economists, some organisers, some accidental heirs, some coalition managers, some mass leaders. Each faced constraints not entirely of their own making. Each also made choices. The purpose here is not to rank them by bravery, wisdom, patriotism, or ideological correctness. It is to reconstruct the pressures around them and the consequences that followed.

The book’s method is narrative, but its duty is evidentiary. Chapters open with scenes because history happened to bodies before it became archives: a soldier at Galwan, refugees from East Pakistan, hostages in Kandahar, commuters in Mumbai, men on Siachen, families leaving Kashmir, diplomats in Tashkent. But scene must never become invention masquerading as evidence. The scenes in this book are built from documented settings and known circumstances, not fabricated private conversations.

India’s story at the crossroads is not only about leaders. It is about the republic’s recurring struggle to join power with judgement. That struggle remains unfinished.

A Note on Sources

This book draws on six broad categories of sources.

The first category is official documentary material: treaty texts, joint statements, Ministry of External Affairs releases, government white papers, parliamentary debates, United Nations Security Council resolutions, court records, and official prime ministerial chronologies. These sources provide the strongest basis for dates, formal decisions, institutional acts, and diplomatic commitments.

The second category is military and security documentation: official histories where available, review committee reports, Ministry of Defence and Press Information Bureau releases, gallantry records, and public statements by senior military officials. These are essential for reconstructing operations such as the 1965 war, Operation Meghdoot, Kargil, surgical strikes, Balakot, and LAC deployments. They must still be read carefully. Official military narratives can clarify sequence and intent, but they can also protect institutional reputation.

The third category is declassified foreign records, especially United States Foreign Relations volumes, National Security Archive material, and diplomatic memoranda. These are particularly useful for the 1962 war, the 1971 Bangladesh crisis, nuclear diplomacy, the Carter-Desai period, and post-Cold War realignments. Foreign archives are not neutral truth. They carry the assumptions, prejudices, intelligence gaps, and policy interests of the governments that produced them. They are valuable because they show what other capitals believed India was doing and what pressures they attempted to apply.

The fourth category is memoir and retrospective testimony: accounts by diplomats, intelligence officers, soldiers, political aides, and journalists. These sources can open doors that official records keep closed. They are also vulnerable to self-justification, selective memory, institutional rivalry, and political loyalty. Claims about covert operations, preventive-strike discussions, backchannel diplomacy, and intelligence failures have therefore been treated with graded confidence.

The fifth category is scholarship: works by historians, economists, political scientists, military analysts, and regional specialists. These sources help place decisions within longer arcs — state formation, non-alignment, nuclear doctrine, insurgency, liberalisation, federalism, and India’s changing place in the world.

The sixth category is contemporary and later journalism. This is indispensable for crises such as the Rubaiya Sayeed kidnapping, the 1990 India-Pakistan scare, Kandahar, 26/11, Doklam, Galwan, the Canada and United States diplomatic-legal controversies, the Maldives crisis, Red Sea deployments, and the 2025 India-Pakistan confrontation. Journalism captures immediacy, public mood, and unofficial claims, but it must be weighed against later records where available.

Several areas remain inherently limited. Cabinet deliberations are not always available. Intelligence records are often classified or selectively disclosed. Casualty figures in communal violence, insurgency, and internal-security operations may be disputed. Covert operations are frequently reconstructed through memoir and unnamed-source reporting. Battlefield damage claims, especially in recent crises, can be politically contested by all sides. The Henderson Brooks–Bhagat report on the 1962 war remains officially unreleased, though leaked versions have circulated. Recent events, including Operation Sindoor and ongoing LAC disengagement arrangements, must be treated as provisional until fuller official and independent documentation becomes available.

The reader should therefore understand the book as a sourced narrative, not as a claim to omniscience. Where the archive is open, it is used. Where it is closed, the closure is acknowledged. Where historians disagree, the disagreement is presented rather than flattened.

Chapter One: Jawaharlal Nehru: The Republic and the Ridge, 1947–1964

Chapter One

Jawaharlal Nehru: The Republic and the Ridge, 1947–1964

At three in the morning, on a Himalayan ridge where breath turned white before it left the mouth, an Indian soldier waited for a dawn he could not yet see. His rifle was cold against his palm. The wireless set coughed with static. Somewhere beyond the dark outline of the mountains, Chinese troops were moving with a discipline and preparation his own side had not imagined fully enough. In Delhi, files had passed from desk to desk, speeches had invoked peace, maps had carried lines that the terrain refused to honour. Here, on the frozen edge of the republic, those lines had become flesh.

Jawaharlal Nehru’s India was born not in calm but in rupture. At midnight on 14–15 August 1947, he stood before the Constituent Assembly and gave a wounded civilisation its most memorable sentence: “At the stroke of the midnight hour,” India would “awake to life and freedom.” The phrase has often been remembered for its grandeur, but the qualification that preceded it mattered just as much: the pledge would be redeemed “not wholly or in full measure.” He knew, even in the ceremonial light of that night, that freedom had arrived with absences, amputations, and unfinished business.

The India Nehru inherited was not yet the India printed in school atlases. Partition had torn Punjab and Bengal open. Refugee columns moved across roads that only weeks earlier had been ordinary routes of commerce and family travel. The British Indian Army had been divided between two new dominions. Princely states still stood in varying degrees of uncertainty, ambition, fear, and defiance. Kashmir was undecided. Junagadh had chosen Pakistan despite geography and demography. Hyderabad imagined a sovereignty of its own in the middle of the Indian Union. Goa, Daman, and Diu remained under Portuguese rule. The republic had been proclaimed before the state had fully gathered itself.

Nehru was not merely India’s first prime minister. For much of his tenure he also held the External Affairs portfolio, making foreign policy unusually concentrated in his own hands. His worldview became, to an unusual degree, the young republic’s diplomatic temperament. Anti-colonialism, non-alignment, Commonwealth pragmatism, Asian solidarity, United Nations faith, and suspicion of military blocs were not ornaments to Nehru’s politics. They were its architecture.

Yet architecture is tested not by drawings but by storms.

The first storm came almost immediately in Kashmir. In October 1947, tribal fighters and Pakistani-supported forces entered Jammu and Kashmir. Maharaja Hari Singh, who had delayed choosing between India and Pakistan, signed the Instrument of Accession to India. Indian troops were flown into the Valley. A war began before either country had properly learned the habits of statehood. The Indian decision to take the matter to the United Nations placed Kashmir inside an international frame from which it never fully emerged. Security Council Resolution 47 of 1948 recommended the withdrawal of tribesmen and Pakistani nationals who had entered the state to fight, followed by steps toward demilitarisation and a plebiscite.

For Nehru, the UN was not simply a courtroom. It was a stage on which a newly independent nation could insist that power must answer to principle. For critics, the reference to the UN became a strategic error: India had surrendered diplomatic initiative after gaining military traction. For defenders, it reflected both the facts of a dangerous war and Nehru’s desire to prevent the dispute from consuming the fragile new state. The result contained both readings. India retained the Kashmir Valley, Jammu, and Ladakh. Pakistan retained territory that would later be called Pakistan-administered Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan. The ceasefire line froze not peace, but argument.

Even as Kashmir burned, New Delhi moved on Junagadh and Hyderabad. Junagadh’s accession to Pakistan was rejected by India on grounds of geography and popular will. Hyderabad, ruled by the Nizam and surrounded by Indian territory, attempted to remain outside the Union. In September 1948, India launched what it called a police action, Operation Polo, and incorporated Hyderabad into the Union. The official story of consolidation sat alongside darker aftermaths: communal violence after the operation remains one of the most contested and painful subjects of the period, with casualty estimates varying sharply and demanding care rather than rhetorical certainty. The state had asserted itself. The nation had not done so without scars.

In these early years, Nehru’s political imagination was pulled between two imperatives that did not always sit comfortably together. He wished India to be a moral voice against colonialism and racial hierarchy. He also had to build a hard state capable of holding territory. Hyderabad required force. Kashmir required troops. Junagadh required pressure. Goa would later require a military operation. The prime minister of non-violence’s heir was learning that sovereignty, once achieved, had to be guarded by instruments Gandhi had distrusted.

Abroad, Nehru sought room for India in a world that demanded alignment. The Korean War gave him one such opening. India did not join combat operations, but it played a prominent diplomatic role in prisoner-of-war repatriation, chaired the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission, and supplied guard personnel. This was the Nehruvian method at its best: present at the crisis, useful to both sides, not absorbed by either camp. India could not yet command the world, but it could mediate within it.

The harder test came from China.

In 1954, India and China signed the Agreement on Trade and Intercourse with the Tibet Region of China. The preamble carried the five principles that came to be known as Panchsheel: respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence. The language seemed made for Nehru’s mind: civilisational, legalistic, hopeful, anti-imperial. It was also incomplete. The agreement dealt with trade and intercourse with Tibet; it did not settle the boundary.

This omission would grow teeth.

When Tibet’s political position changed under Chinese control, India’s assumptions began to fray. The Dalai Lama’s flight to India in 1959 deepened Chinese suspicion and Indian unease. Border incidents at Longju and Kongka Pass revealed that the frontier was no longer a cartographic abstraction. In Delhi, the Forward Policy would eventually place small Indian posts in disputed areas. In the mountains, those posts were sometimes isolated, under-supplied, and vulnerable. A policy conceived as assertion could become exposure.

Nehru’s China policy remains one of the most disputed legacies in Indian strategic history. Some historians and strategists argue that he underestimated Chinese intentions, allowed slogans of Asian solidarity to cloud military judgement, and neglected infrastructure and preparedness. Others contend that the crisis grew from incompatible maps, inherited imperial frontiers, Tibet’s transformation, Chinese consolidation, and a young state’s limited resources. The still-classified Henderson Brooks–Bhagat report has become a symbol of this unresolved reckoning. Its public status remains contested: the Government of India has not officially released it, though a leaked version has circulated, and scholars have debated its implications for military and political responsibility.

The tragedy of 1962 was that many of these arguments became clear only after men had died.

Before that humiliation, Nehru’s foreign policy had known moments of undeniable stature. During the Suez Crisis of 1956, India opposed the Anglo-French-Israeli intervention in Egypt, aligning its rhetoric with anti-colonial principle. Yet the same year, India’s response to Soviet intervention in Hungary was more guarded, creating the charge that non-alignment was not always equidistance but sometimes selective caution. Nehru’s defenders saw the pressures of geography, military weakness, and Cold War dependence. His critics saw moral asymmetry. The charge would follow India’s foreign policy for decades: could a postcolonial state speak universally while calculating locally?

With Pakistan, Nehru’s most durable settlement was not over land but water. On 19 September 1960, in Karachi, Nehru, Pakistani President Ayub Khan, and the World Bank’s representative signed the Indus Waters Treaty. The treaty allocated the eastern rivers to India and the western rivers primarily to Pakistan, subject to specified Indian uses.

The treaty was both practical and symbolic. It did not solve Kashmir. It did not produce trust between India and Pakistan. But it showed that even adversarial states could create a working mechanism around a shared necessity. Water forced a realism that territory had denied. In later decades, the treaty would acquire an almost mythic reputation for surviving wars and crises, though its endurance would itself come under strain in the twenty-first century. The larger lesson was less sentimental: peace sometimes survives not because countries like each other, but because institutions make hostility administrable.

Goa posed the opposite problem. Portugal refused to leave. India argued that European colonial possession on the subcontinent had become an anachronism after independence. Diplomatic efforts failed. Operation Vijay was swift. Portugal’s rule collapsed. At the UN, a Western-backed draft resolution called for ceasefire, withdrawal of Indian forces, and resumption of negotiations. The Soviet Union vetoed it.

Here was Nehru in apparent contradiction. The man who invoked the UN over Kashmir accepted Soviet protection at the UN over Goa. The leader of non-alignment used great-power veto when it suited India’s anti-colonial claim. But statesmen often live inside contradictions that pamphleteers can avoid. Nehru’s argument was that Goa was not an ordinary territorial dispute but unfinished decolonisation. Western governments, especially those allied to Portugal, were not persuaded. Many Indians were. The liberation of Goa gave the country an emotional satisfaction that legal caution alone could not have supplied.

Still, the pattern was becoming visible. Nehru trusted law, but not always enough to forgo force. He distrusted military blocs, but accepted Soviet diplomatic shielding. He believed in Asian fraternity, but faced war with China. He wanted Kashmir’s future to be morally defensible, but the UN process hardened into stalemate. His career did not fail because it lacked principles. It suffered because principles had to cross terrain.

The terrain in 1962 was cruel.

By October that year, the India-China dispute had moved beyond diplomatic notes. Chinese forces attacked in both the western and eastern sectors. In the eastern theatre, then known to India as NEFA, the collapse was severe. In Ladakh, Indian positions also came under heavy pressure. The war exposed failures in intelligence, logistics, equipment, mountain warfare preparation, and civil-military decision-making. After 1962, India could no longer treat defence planning as secondary to developmental aspiration.

Nehru’s public voice after the defeat carried strain beneath formality. In the Lok Sabha on 10 December 1962, he remarked with dry weariness that since the war began there was “no lack of experts on military strategy” in the country. It was a defensive sentence, but also a human one. The prime minister who had once embodied national confidence was now surrounded by advice, anger, grief, and hindsight. In private and diplomatic communication, the shift was starker. In a letter to President John F. Kennedy during the crisis, Nehru stressed that assistance sought from the United States would be used “entirely for resistance against the Chinese.”

For a leader who had built India’s international identity around non-alignment, the appeal carried an unmistakable humiliation. It did not mean non-alignment had ended. It meant non-alignment had encountered emergency. The border had done what critics had failed to do: it had forced Nehru to measure aspiration against capacity.

The nation’s pulse changed after 1962. The press, Parliament, and public conversation turned harsher. The earlier moral confidence of anti-colonial diplomacy gave way to questions about maps, rifles, roads, air power, command, and ministerial responsibility. V. K. Krishna Menon, Nehru’s controversial defence minister and close ally, resigned. The armed forces entered a period of expansion and reform. Defence spending rose. A country that had imagined development and security as competing claims discovered that neglecting one could imperil the other.

The emotional cost was not only institutional. Nehru had tied much of his public life to the belief that newly free Asian nations could avoid the brutal habits of European power politics. China’s attack did not merely defeat Indian formations; it wounded an idea. That wound explains the lasting bitterness of 1962 in Indian memory. Military defeat alone can be studied. Betrayal, once felt, becomes folklore.

Yet a fair verdict cannot reduce Nehru to 1962. His tenure created diplomatic habits that endured: suspicion of alliance entanglement, belief in strategic autonomy, preference for negotiated settlement where possible, willingness to use force for territorial consolidation where deemed necessary, and investment in international legitimacy. He left unresolved disputes, but he also left an India that had survived Partition, integrated most princely states, built parliamentary institutions, spoken beyond its material power, and begun to define itself as neither camp follower nor hermit state.

His ledger is therefore neither hagiography nor indictment. On Kashmir, he achieved military stabilisation but accepted an international process that later governments would resist. On Hyderabad and Goa, he consolidated the Union but left historical controversies over force, legitimacy, and aftermath. On Korea and Congo, India gained diplomatic stature through UN roles. On Suez and Hungary, non-alignment revealed both moral ambition and strategic inconsistency. On the Indus Waters Treaty, he accepted material compromise for durable risk reduction. On China, his policy ended in battlefield defeat and a deep strategic correction.

The verdict of history remains divided because Nehru’s failures and achievements emerged from the same temperament. His faith in law gave India international standing; it also made him slow to recognise when law lacked enforcement. His anti-colonialism gave India moral authority; it also encouraged him to see some conflicts through the older lens of empire when new power realities had changed the field. His centralisation of foreign policy gave coherence; it also reduced the friction that sometimes prevents error. His suspicion of militarism protected a fragile democracy from barracks politics; it also contributed to a dangerous underestimation of military preparedness.

In the final years of his life, Nehru seemed older than his age. The China war had not removed him from office, but it had altered the light around him. The Parliament still heard his voice. Foreign visitors still called. Files still came. Yet the man who had spoken of India awakening to freedom had now seen the republic wake to vulnerability.

On 27 May 1964, Nehru died. The country mourned not simply a prime minister but a founding presence. In the north, the mountains remained. In the west, Kashmir remained. Across the rivers, Pakistan remained. Beyond the Himalaya, China remained. The institutions Nehru had built would now be tested by men who had not stood at midnight in 1947, men who inherited not the dream’s first music but its unfinished accounts.

And in the silence after the mourning, another question began to move through Delhi: could a smaller, quieter man named Lal Bahadur Shastri hold together a nation that had just discovered how cold the high ridges could be?

Chapter Two: Lal Bahadur Shastri: The Small Man and the Long Border, 1964–1966

Chapter Two

Lal Bahadur Shastri: The Small Man and the Long Border, 1964–1966

The room in South Block did not need grandeur to become a war room. A table, a map, a telephone, a few tired men, and a prime minister whose physical presence seemed almost to disappear into his white khadi were enough. Outside, Delhi was hot, hungry, and watchful. Inside, reports moved from Kashmir to Punjab, from the Rann of Kutch to Lahore, from the frontier to the United Nations. Lal Bahadur Shastri listened quietly. He had inherited a nation still bruised by China. Pakistan believed that bruise had not healed.

He was not the kind of man history expects at moments of crisis. There was no theatrical authority about him, no aristocratic command, no oratorical sweep of the Nehruvian kind. He was slight, soft-spoken, austere, and almost deliberately unadorned. Yet the India he inherited in June 1964 required steel in a modest vessel. Jawaharlal Nehru had left behind institutions, anxieties, an unresolved Kashmir, a damaged military confidence, food shortages, a planning state under strain, and a public still trying to understand the humiliation of 1962. Shastri’s first burden was not to invent India’s strategic posture, but to restore its nerve.

He became prime minister after the 1962 China defeat and Nehru’s death, while India was rebuilding military credibility, managing food insecurity, and facing a Pakistan led by Ayub Khan that assessed India as vulnerable. That assessment mattered. War often begins in the mind before it begins on a map. If Pakistan’s leadership concluded that India’s morale had cracked, then Kashmir appeared not as a frozen dispute but as an opportunity.

Before Kashmir ignited fully, the Rann of Kutch became the testing ground.

The Rann was unlike the Himalayan battlefields that had haunted India since 1962. It was salt, heat, emptiness, mirage. A landscape where land and water shifted in meaning, where patrol lines could become claims and claims could become skirmishes. In early 1965, Indian and Pakistani forces clashed there. It was not yet a full war, but it carried the scent of rehearsal. British mediation helped produce a ceasefire and arbitration. The later tribunal award gave most of the disputed territory to India and a smaller portion to Pakistan, making the episode a diplomatic resolution rather than a battlefield transformation. For Shastri, however, the Rann had offered an early warning: Pakistan was willing to probe.

The greater crisis arrived in Kashmir.

Pakistan’s Operation Gibraltar was built on a wager: infiltrate men into Jammu and Kashmir, stir insurgency, and force India into a politically and militarily disadvantageous position. The plan assumed that Kashmiris would rise in sufficient numbers, that India would be constrained in response, and that the aftermath of 1962 had weakened Indian will. In August 1965, infiltrators crossed into Kashmir. Local response did not conform neatly to Pakistani expectations. Indian forces moved to cut off infiltration routes, and the conflict escalated.

Then came Operation Grand Slam. On 1 September 1965, Pakistan launched a major offensive in the Chhamb-Jaurian sector aimed at Akhnoor. The objective was strategically grave: Akhnoor was a communications node whose loss could threaten Indian positions in Jammu and Kashmir. Indian accounts later described the Pakistani plan as an attack intended to capture Akhnoor and sever communications and supply routes to Indian troops.

In Delhi, the question became stark. Should India confine the conflict to Kashmir, fighting where Pakistan had chosen the ground, or widen the war? Shastri authorised the wider response. On 6 September 1965, Indian forces crossed the international border near Lahore. This was the decisive political act of his premiership. It announced that aggression in Kashmir would not necessarily remain geographically contained. It also meant that India was prepared to accept the risks of full-scale war.

For a prime minister often remembered through the gentleness of “Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan,” the decision was severe. The slogan itself should not be domesticated into mere patriotism. It joined two anxieties of the moment: the soldier at the front and the farmer in the field. India was fighting under the shadow of food insecurity. The state needed courage in the trenches and grain in the granaries. The war and the kitchen were not separate theatres.

The fighting was fierce. In Punjab, the battles around Lahore and the Ichhogil Canal became symbols of advance and resistance. At Asal Uttar, Indian forces halted a Pakistani armoured thrust, destroying or capturing large numbers of Patton tanks and giving the battlefield a place in public memory. In the air, the Indian Air Force and Pakistan Air Force fought with an intensity unseen in the first Kashmir war. On the ground, both armies paid heavily. Casualty figures vary by source and methodology, and any responsible narrative must resist turning uncertain numbers into rhetorical weapons. What was beyond dispute was the scale of national mobilisation and the psychological break from 1962. India had not collapsed.

Shastri’s public tone during the war was steady, not flamboyant. His authority came from restraint. Where Nehru had often educated the nation through long exposition, Shastri compressed resolve into plain speech. In a broadcast during the crisis, he told Indians that force would be met with force. The sentence worked because it did not strain for grandeur. It sounded like a moral rule, not a flourish.

Yet even battlefield resolve had to pass through diplomatic corridors. The United Nations intervened. Security Council Resolution 211 demanded that a ceasefire take effect on 22 September 1965 and called for withdrawal of armed personnel to the positions held before 5 August 1965. The war had become not only an India-Pakistan contest but a Cold War concern. Washington and Moscow did not want escalation in South Asia. China’s shadow also mattered. Pakistan had sought to exploit Indian vulnerability after 1962; India could not ignore the possibility of a wider strategic complication.

The ceasefire produced a familiar Indian discomfort: soldiers had fought, territory had been taken, and diplomats now required withdrawal. The tension between battlefield sacrifice and negotiated settlement is one of the oldest pains of war. Shastri accepted the ceasefire. His critics could ask whether India had converted military resilience into sufficient political gain. His defenders could answer that India had disproved Pakistani assumptions, preserved Kashmir, and avoided a larger war under dangerous international pressure. Both arguments belong in the record.

The institutional legacy of 1965 was immediate. The war revealed the need for a specialised border force. On 1 December 1965, the Border Security Force was raised, with K. F. Rustamji as its first chief. This was one of the clearest examples of conflict producing architecture. The border was no longer to be treated simply as a line policed by dispersed arrangements. It required an institution with a dedicated identity.

The nation’s pulse during Shastri’s war differed sharply from that of 1962. There was anxiety, certainly. There was censorship, propaganda, rumour, and grief. But there was also the sensation of recovered balance. India had absorbed an assault and answered it. For a public that had lived through the shock of Chinese advances in NEFA and Ladakh, the very fact of resistance mattered. Newspapers carried battlefield reports, speeches, photographs of soldiers, and the language of sacrifice. Railway stations, bazaars, mohallas, and university campuses became spaces of wartime conversation. Families listened to radio bulletins with the peculiar silence that war creates: everyone present, no one quite able to speak freely.

At the centre stood a prime minister who did not dominate the imagination by force of personality. That was perhaps his strength. Shastri’s austerity allowed many Indians to project trust upon him. He did not appear to be performing command. He appeared to be bearing it.

Then came Tashkent.

In January 1966, Shastri travelled to the Soviet Union for talks with Pakistan’s President Ayub Khan under the mediation of Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin. The Tashkent Declaration was signed on 10 January 1966. Its terms required both sides to withdraw armed personnel to positions held before 5 August 1965, restore diplomatic relations, and continue efforts to normalise relations.

The declaration was a diplomatic resolution, but not an emotional one. In India, many accepted it as the necessary price of peace. Others felt that military gains, including hard-won positions, had been traded away. This recurrent grievance would attach itself especially to Haji Pir, captured by Indian forces during the war and returned under the settlement. For military men who had seen the cost of taking ground, withdrawal could feel like erasure. For diplomats, withdrawal was the mechanism that prevented the next war from beginning immediately.

Shastri stood at the centre of that contradiction: victory without conquest, resilience without settlement, honour without finality.

And then, in the early hours after Tashkent, he died.

His death on 11 January 1966 transformed the declaration from a diplomatic document into a national memory. The prime minister who had gone to negotiate peace did not return alive. In India, shock mingled with suspicion, sorrow, and the strange vulnerability produced when a leader dies away from home. The official record says he died of a heart attack. Public speculation has persisted across decades, though no responsible historian can convert suspicion into fact without evidence. What matters to the narrative is the emotional rupture: India had lost its second prime minister less than two years after losing its first, and this time the death came immediately after war.

The ledger of Shastri’s tenure is compact because his time in office was brief. But compact does not mean slight. He stabilised national morale after 1962. He authorised expansion of the 1965 war across the international border, changing the logic of Pakistan’s Kashmir strategy. He accepted a UN-backed ceasefire and Soviet-mediated settlement. His government created the BSF, a major institutional response to border vulnerability. He governed amid food stress and war expenditure, making the soldier-farmer pairing not a slogan of convenience but a description of national survival.

The economic pressure was severe. Defence demands rose at a time when India was struggling with food shortages and dependence on external assistance. War did not occur in a vacuum of patriotic feeling; it consumed fuel, foreign exchange, administrative attention, and fiscal room. The Green Revolution was still emerging as policy and technological transformation. In Shastri’s India, national security and food security were twins born of anxiety.

Historians and strategists have generally treated 1965 not as a decisive territorial victory but as a war that defeated Pakistan’s assumptions. Pakistan did not trigger the expected uprising in Kashmir. India did not remain confined to the theatre Pakistan had selected. The Indian Army, still recovering from the trauma of 1962, demonstrated operational resilience. At the same time, the war exposed weaknesses in intelligence, coordination, equipment, and higher defence management. It ended without resolving Kashmir and without establishing lasting peace. The ceasefire line remained tense. Pakistan’s search for strategic parity continued. India’s defence rebuilding accelerated.

Shastri’s historical reputation rests partly on what he did and partly on what he seemed to be. He represented a form of power that did not need spectacle. That has made him easy to praise sentimentally and difficult to analyse rigorously. The sentimental Shastri is the modest patriot, the man of “Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan,” the leader who died after Tashkent. The historical Shastri is more demanding: a prime minister who widened a war, accepted external mediation, strengthened border institutions, and governed a hungry country under military pressure.

Neither image cancels the other.

In the end, his tenure answered one question and left another. Could India recover from the psychological wound of 1962? Yes, at least enough to stand its ground in 1965. Could India convert battlefield resilience into a stable settlement with Pakistan? Not yet.

When Shastri’s body returned from Tashkent, the country mourned a man whose small frame had carried a heavy republic. But grief leaves little time in politics. Inside the Congress party, succession began. Outside India, adversaries watched. In the fields, food remained uncertain. On the borders, soldiers stayed alert.

And in Delhi, a woman whom many senior men believed they could manage was moving towards the office where Nehru had dreamed and Shastri had decided. History was preparing to test Indira Gandhi before it understood her.

Chapter Three: Indira Gandhi: Fire in the East, Shadow in the State, 1966–1977

Chapter Three

Indira Gandhi: Fire in the East, Shadow in the State, 1966–1977

In March 1971, the refugees began arriving before India had decided what history would call the crisis. They came over the border from East Pakistan carrying children, brass pots, torn documents, and wounds that could not be hidden beneath cloth. In the camps of West Bengal, Tripura, Assam, Meghalaya, and Bihar, the air thickened with sweat, rain, smoke, illness, and fear. A woman who had once known the geography of her village now knew only the direction of escape. A child learned that a country could vanish before he understood what a country was. In Delhi, Indira Gandhi read reports that turned human misery into numbers. The numbers kept growing.

She had entered the prime minister’s office in January 1966 under the shadow of death. Lal Bahadur Shastri had died in Tashkent. Jawaharlal Nehru, her father, had died less than two years earlier. India had endured war with China, war with Pakistan, food insecurity, foreign exchange strain, and the psychological exhaustion of a republic repeatedly tested before it had found its full strength. India was recovering from the 1965 war, coping with food insecurity, rebuilding defence capacity, and navigating a Cold War environment in which Pakistan had links with the United States and China while India remained formally non-aligned but increasingly close to the Soviet Union.

Indira Gandhi’s early image deceived many contemporaries. Congress elders had helped elevate her partly because they believed she would be manageable. The phrase “goongi gudiya”, attributed in political folklore to critics who underestimated her, survives because it captures more than insult; it captures misreading. Her authority did not arrive fully formed. It hardened. The India she governed in 1966 was hungry, fractious, and strategically uncertain. By 1971, she would preside over the most decisive military victory in independent India’s history. By 1975, she would preside over the Emergency. The arc was not accidental. Power, in her hands, became at once instrument of national assertion and instrument of domestic centralisation.

The first military signal of the new era came not from Pakistan but from China. In September 1967 at Nathu La and in October at Cho La, Indian and Chinese troops clashed along the Sikkim-Tibet frontier. Five years after the humiliation of 1962, the encounters carried disproportionate psychological weight. Indian accounts have generally treated the clashes as tactical successes, a sign that the army had recovered confidence and that the border would no longer be defended with the same fragility exposed in NEFA and Ladakh. The fighting did not settle the boundary. It did something subtler: it challenged the permanence of the 1962 memory.

The other institutional lesson of the decade was intelligence. In 1968, India created the Research and Analysis Wing, separating external intelligence from the Intelligence Bureau. The move is often associated with R. N. Kao, whose name would become inseparable from India’s covert and strategic apparatus. The decision reflected a recognition that the old arrangements had proved inadequate for a country whose dangers were now external, internal, ideological, military, and subcontinental all at once. RAW’s creation under Indira Gandhi marked a deepening of the security state. Its full story remains partly hidden, as intelligence histories always do, but its institutional meaning is clear: the republic wanted eyes beyond its borders.

Then came East Pakistan.

The crisis began with politics before it became war. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman’s Awami League had won a democratic mandate in Pakistan’s 1970 elections. West Pakistan’s ruling establishment refused to transfer power. On the night of 25 March 1971, the Pakistani army launched Operation Searchlight in East Pakistan. Repression followed on a vast scale. Millions fled into India. The moral, humanitarian, fiscal, and strategic burden fell on New Delhi. The refugee crisis was not a metaphor. It was a logistical emergency. Camps had to be built, food supplied, disease contained, and local political tensions managed. The Indian state absorbed not only displaced people but also the pressure of deciding whether suffering across a border had become a national-security crisis.

Indira Gandhi did not rush publicly into war. She travelled abroad, including to Western capitals, to make India’s case. She sought to convince the world that the crisis could not be reduced to an internal matter of Pakistan. The refugees were on Indian soil. The violence had crossed the border through its consequences. Yet global sympathy did not translate easily into action. The United States, under President Richard Nixon and Henry Kissinger, saw Pakistan through the prism of Cold War diplomacy and the opening to China. China was Pakistan’s partner. India had to calculate not only Rawalpindi’s moves but Washington’s and Beijing’s.

On 9 August 1971, India and the Soviet Union signed the Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation. The treaty’s text spoke in the language of peace, sovereignty, non-interference, and cooperation, but its strategic context was unmistakable. It gave India diplomatic insurance at a moment when the Bangladesh crisis was moving toward war.

For a country formally committed to non-alignment, the treaty invited scrutiny. Was this a departure from Nehru’s doctrine or an adaptation of it? Critics saw drift toward Moscow. Defenders saw strategic autonomy expressed through necessity: India had not joined a Soviet bloc, they argued; it had secured room to act in a hostile configuration of powers. The distinction mattered because India’s policy vocabulary had to preserve continuity even when reality demanded adjustment.

On 3 December 1971, Pakistan launched air strikes on Indian airfields in the west. The war was now open. India moved on both fronts, but the decisive theatre lay in the east. The Indian Army, aided by the Mukti Bahini and supported by air and naval operations, advanced rapidly toward Dhaka. The campaign was short, mobile, and politically clear in its objective: force the surrender of Pakistani forces in East Pakistan and enable Bangladesh’s emergence.

India recognised Bangladesh on 6 December 1971, ten days before liberation. On 16 December 1971, Pakistan’s Eastern Command surrendered in Dhaka. Bangladesh was born.

Few events in modern Indian history have produced such a concentrated convergence of military success, diplomatic preparation, humanitarian justification, and public emotion. For Indian citizens who had lived through 1962 and 1965, the surrender in Dhaka carried the force of reversal. The army that had been written off by some after China had executed one of the most effective campaigns of the postcolonial world. The prime minister who had been underestimated by her party had become the face of national power.

But victory has its own dangers. It can make later restraint harder to understand.

After the war, India held more than 90,000 Pakistani prisoners of war. It had battlefield advantage and diplomatic leverage. In July 1972, Indira Gandhi and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto signed the Simla Agreement. The agreement committed India and Pakistan to settle differences through bilateral negotiations or other mutually agreed peaceful means, and it converted the ceasefire line in Jammu and Kashmir into the Line of Control.

The Simla Agreement remains one of the most debated diplomatic settlements in Indian history. Its defenders argue that Indira Gandhi avoided humiliating Pakistan to the point of permanent revanchism, secured bilateralism as the framework for Kashmir, and brought prisoners and territory into a manageable peace. Its critics argue that India failed to convert overwhelming advantage into a final settlement on Kashmir or recognition of the Line of Control as an international border. The dispute cannot be resolved by hindsight alone. Bhutto arrived at Simla politically vulnerable but not powerless; Pakistan’s future stability, the prisoners’ fate, international pressure, and India’s own desire to consolidate victory all weighed on the negotiation. Indira Gandhi chose settlement over maximal extraction.

The human behind the power is hard to recover in such moments because official photographs flatten history into poses. Yet the very setting of Simla — the hill station air, the late-night negotiation, the personalities of Gandhi and Bhutto circling each other across grievance and necessity — suggests the intimacy of statecraft after war. They were not merely signing paper. They were deciding how much defeat a state could absorb without becoming more dangerous.

In 1974, Indira Gandhi took India across another threshold. On 18 May, India conducted its first nuclear test at Pokhran in Rajasthan. India described it as a peaceful nuclear explosion. The test, later popularly associated with the name “Smiling Buddha,” made India the first country outside the five recognised nuclear-weapon states to demonstrate such capability after the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty framework had begun hardening global nuclear hierarchy.

The word “peaceful” did heavy political work. It allowed India to claim technological achievement and sovereign scientific capability without formally declaring a weapons programme. For supporters, Pokhran-I was a necessary assertion against a discriminatory nuclear order, especially after China’s 1964 nuclear test and the strategic shocks of the previous decade. For critics, it invited technology denial, suspicion, and regional nuclear competition. Both outcomes followed. India gained stature among some at home and in the developing world as a country unwilling to accept permanent technological subordination. It also faced restrictions and distrust from nuclear-supplier states, helping shape the regime that would later become the Nuclear Suppliers Group.

The economic ledger of these years cannot be separated from the strategic one. The Bangladesh refugee crisis imposed major fiscal and logistical burdens. The war itself consumed resources, though its swift conclusion limited prolonged cost. The emergence of Bangladesh removed the immediate refugee burden but did not end regional instability. Pokhran brought prestige but also technological constraint. Food production improved through the Green Revolution, but economic stress, inflation, labour unrest, and political agitation continued. The image of Indira Gandhi as the leader of decisive victory coexisted with a society increasingly restless under centralised authority.

The incorporation of Sikkim in 1975 was another act of territorial-political consolidation. After political turmoil in the Himalayan kingdom, constitutional change, and a referendum process, Sikkim became a state of India. China objected for years before later adjusting its position. Within the Indian strategic view, Sikkim’s incorporation secured a sensitive Himalayan space between Nepal, Bhutan, Tibet, and India’s northeast. Within the political history of the region, it remains a subject requiring care: monarchy, democratic agitation, Indian influence, local aspiration, and geopolitics all intersected.

By then, however, the centre of Indian politics had darkened. In June 1975, after the Allahabad High Court found Indira Gandhi guilty of electoral malpractice and invalidated her election, she imposed the Emergency. Civil liberties were suspended, opposition leaders jailed, censorship imposed, and the authority of the prime minister’s office expanded dramatically. Though this chapter’s geopolitical frame centres on war and foreign policy, the Emergency cannot be treated as a separate domestic appendix. It changed the meaning of state power. The same centralising instinct that had enabled rapid strategic decision-making also turned inward against democratic dissent.

Neutrality requires precision here. The Emergency was defended by its supporters at the time as necessary to restore order amid political agitation and administrative paralysis. Its critics, including many later historians and constitutional scholars, have treated it as independent India’s gravest democratic rupture. The record of arrests, censorship, coercive sterilisation drives, and institutional intimidation gives the criticism substantial documentary weight. Yet the historian must resist psychological simplification. Indira Gandhi was not merely a liberator of Bangladesh who became authoritarian, nor merely an authoritarian who happened to win a war. She was both the prime minister who presided over India’s greatest external military success and the leader under whom democratic freedoms were suspended. The discomfort is the point.

The nation’s pulse between 1971 and 1977 therefore moved through extremes: refugee sympathy, wartime mobilisation, victory euphoria, nuclear pride, economic discontent, fear, silence, and finally electoral judgement. In 1977, when elections were held, Indian voters removed her from office. That result remains one of the republic’s most powerful correctives. The Emergency had shown how far central power could go. The election showed that it could still be turned out.

The verdict of history on Indira Gandhi’s first tenure is necessarily double-edged. Strategists have credited her with aligning military operations, diplomacy, intelligence, and political objective in 1971 with rare effectiveness. The Indo-Soviet Treaty, recognition of Bangladesh, management of great-power pressure, and swift campaign in the east form a coherent strategic achievement. The Simla Agreement remains contested precisely because India had so much leverage and chose a restrained settlement. Pokhran-I asserted technological sovereignty while inviting nuclear isolation. Sikkim consolidated India’s Himalayan position while leaving interpretive disputes over process and agency. RAW’s creation strengthened external intelligence capacity. The 1967 clashes helped restore confidence after 1962.

The costs were equally real. The Bangladesh victory did not settle the Pakistan problem. Simla did not resolve Kashmir. Pokhran did not create a stable nuclear order in South Asia. The centralised security state acquired habits that would later shape Punjab, Kashmir, and Sri Lanka. The Emergency damaged institutions whose strength was itself part of national security. A country can win wars and still wound its constitutional soul.

Indira Gandhi’s first tenure forces the reader to hold two images together. In one, she is the leader standing at the apex of a state that helped midwife Bangladesh, faced down pressure, and gave India a new sense of strategic possibility. In the other, she is the prime minister under whom citizens lowered their voices, newspapers carried blank spaces, and the state entered homes and bodies with coercive ambition. Neither image is propaganda. Both are history.

When she left office in 1977, defeated by voters she had once governed under Emergency rule, many assumed her age had passed. The Janata experiment promised restoration, morality, decentralisation, and a gentler state. Pakistan watched. China watched. Washington watched. Moscow watched.

And somewhere in the machinery of Indian intelligence, diplomacy, and politics, files remained open: Pakistan’s nuclear programme, Afghanistan’s instability, Punjab’s unease, Sri Lanka’s Tamil question, and the high glacier where India and Pakistan had not yet begun the coldest contest of all.

Chapter Four: Morarji Desai: Morality After Midnight, 1977–1979

Chapter Four

Morarji Desai: Morality After Midnight, 1977–1979

On the morning after fear loosens, a city does not at once become free. Delhi in 1977 carried the residue of the Emergency in its walls, its newsrooms, its police stations, its party offices, its whispered habits. Files had been opened on citizens. Newspapers had learned caution. Politicians had emerged from prison older than when they entered. In the prime minister’s residence, the new occupant was an old man with severe habits and sharper certainties. Morarji Desai had come to power not through revolution but through the ballot box, and yet the mandate felt cleansing. The country wanted air.

The Janata victory of 1977 was one of the republic’s great democratic reversals. Indira Gandhi, victorious in war six years earlier and dominant through Emergency rule, had been defeated. The people had removed a government that had suspended their liberties. Desai became prime minister at eighty-one, carrying into office a reputation for personal austerity, administrative discipline, and Gandhian-inflected moral conviction. He was not a poet of the nation like Nehru, nor a wartime symbol like Shastri, nor a centralising force like Indira Gandhi. He was a corrective.

Correctives, however, must govern the world they inherit.

Desai came to power after the Emergency and after India’s 1974 nuclear test. His government sought détente with Pakistan and China and better ties with the United States while resisting full alignment with the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. At home, he represented restoration. Abroad, he had to manage consequences created before him: Pokhran, Pakistan’s nuclear effort, China’s unfinished border, the Soviet relationship, American suspicion, and a subcontinent still defined by the 1971 war.

The first major diplomatic pressure point was nuclear.

India’s 1974 test had unsettled the global non-proliferation order. The United States, under President Jimmy Carter, made nuclear restraint a central part of its foreign policy. Tarapur, the nuclear power station built with American cooperation, became a site of tension. Washington wanted assurances regarding U.S.-supplied nuclear material and safeguards. India wanted fuel supply commitments honoured without accepting what it saw as discriminatory constraints. Desai, personally opposed to nuclear weapons, might have seemed a natural partner for Carter. The two men shared a moral vocabulary. Yet nuclear diplomacy often punishes moral similarity because law, pride, technology, and memory intrude.

Desai publicly stated that India would not conduct further nuclear explosions or make nuclear weapons. This was Desai at his most distinctive. Unlike Indira Gandhi, he did not seek prestige from nuclear demonstration. His language returned to the earlier Indian position that nuclear energy should serve peaceful purposes and that nuclear disarmament, if genuine, had to be universal. But he did not accept the Non-Proliferation Treaty as legitimate hierarchy. He could reject weapons and still reject nuclear tutelage. That distinction has marked Indian policy from Nehru through later governments: India’s criticism of the nuclear order has often been less a defence of proliferation than an attack on permanent privilege.

President Carter visited India in January 1978. The symbolism was powerful. An American president came to a post-Emergency India and spoke of democracy. A prime minister who had suffered imprisonment under Indira Gandhi’s government stood as evidence that Indian democracy had corrected itself. Yet beneath the ceremony, Tarapur remained unresolved. Carter later pledged, in the joint communiqué during Desai’s U.S. visit, to make every effort consistent with American law to maintain fuel supplies for Tarapur and continue nuclear cooperation.

The phrase “consistent with American law” was the hinge. India heard qualification where it wanted assurance. The United States heard non-proliferation responsibility where India heard retroactive pressure. The crisis did not explode, but it did not settle cleanly either. It produced tactical relief and strategic mistrust.

Desai’s Pakistan policy was equally marked by restraint. He sought improved relations with General Zia-ul-Haq. The memory of 1971 still hung over Pakistan’s military and political imagination. Bangladesh had broken the two-wing state. India held the prestige of victory. Yet Pakistan’s nuclear programme, centred in part around Kahuta, was gathering urgency. Here, Desai’s legacy becomes controversial.

Some intelligence memoirs and later accounts allege that Desai, distrustful of covert operations and eager for détente, damaged Indian intelligence sources related to Pakistan’s nuclear programme. The available public evidence remains insufficient for a definitive historical verdict. A responsible account must therefore distinguish between three things: Desai’s documented preference for improved relations with Pakistan, India’s concern about Pakistan’s nuclear trajectory, and contested claims about intelligence compromise.

The temptation in political history is to turn disputed intelligence stories into morality plays. Desai the naïve moralist. Indira the hard realist. Zia the cunning soldier. Such portraits are seductive and often false in proportion to their neatness. Desai’s suspicion of covert activism did fit his public character. He believed in open conduct and moral rectitude. But the scale and operational consequence of any reduction in RAW activity remain obscured by secrecy and memoir. What can be said safely is that Pakistan’s nuclear trajectory continued, and India did not stop it.

China offered another opening and another disappointment.

After the 1962 war, India-China relations had remained cold for years. Ambassadorial relations were restored in 1976, just before Desai’s government came to power. The Janata government sought cautious normalisation. External Affairs Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee travelled to China in February 1979, the first ministerial-level visit between the two countries in more than sixteen years.

The visit was symbolically heavy. Vajpayee was not yet the prime minister he would become decades later, but he already possessed the political imagination and verbal instinct that made him one of India’s distinctive public figures. He met Chinese leaders at a time when both countries were testing whether the wound of 1962 could be managed without being healed. Then China invaded Vietnam. The news broke during the visit. Vajpayee cut the trip short.

The diplomatic irony was sharp. India’s foreign minister had gone to Beijing to normalise relations; China chose that moment to launch a war against Vietnam, a Soviet partner and a country with which India had sympathy. The visit did not collapse India-China diplomacy entirely, but it exposed how limited the thaw remained. The border was not settled. Strategic trust was absent. China’s regional conduct could still disrupt bilateral atmospherics in an instant.

Afghanistan, meanwhile, was beginning to darken the horizon. The Saur Revolution of 1978 brought a communist government to Kabul. The full Soviet invasion would occur after Desai had left office, but the warning signs were visible. Afghanistan mattered to India through Pakistan. Any instability there could elevate Pakistan’s importance to Washington and reshape the military balance in South Asia. Desai’s government, committed to non-alignment and improved Western ties, still had to preserve the Soviet relationship. This was the old Indian problem in a new form: remain independent without becoming irrelevant to either side.

At home, the Janata government’s greatest challenge was itself. It contained socialists, Bharatiya Jana Sangh veterans, Congress(O) leaders, Gandhian conservatives, anti-Emergency activists, and personal rivals united more by opposition to Indira Gandhi than by a coherent programme. Desai’s administrative rectitude could not dissolve faction. The moral authority of having restored democracy did not automatically produce governing cohesion.

The nation’s pulse during the Desai years was therefore complex. There was relief after the Emergency, yes. There was also impatience. The public had voted against authoritarianism, but it still needed food, employment, stability, and credible governance. The press recovered its voice. Parliament recovered some of its bite. Political prisoners returned to public life. But the Janata Party’s internal quarrels increasingly consumed the energy that might have gone into reform.

In security terms, Desai’s tenure saw no major war. This absence should not be mistaken for insignificance. Periods without war often shape the terms on which later wars are fought or avoided. His government lowered some tensions, sought détente, resisted nuclear pressure without repeating nuclear tests, and reopened diplomatic possibilities with China. It also left unresolved the nuclear rivalry with Pakistan, the structural ambiguity of Afghanistan, and the limits of intelligence transparency.

The ledger is therefore subdued but consequential.

On nuclear policy, Desai refused weaponisation and further testing, yet rejected discriminatory non-proliferation constraints. On Tarapur, his government obtained limited relief but not trust. On Pakistan, he sought dialogue and reduced overt hostility, but Pakistan’s nuclear programme continued. On China, he attempted normalisation, only to have the effort disrupted by Beijing’s war with Vietnam. On Afghanistan, his government witnessed the first tremors of a crisis that would soon transform U.S.-Pakistan relations. On the security architecture, his reported distrust of RAW and covert activism remains part of the historical debate, but public documentation is limited.

Historians have often treated Desai as a transitional prime minister: important for democratic restoration, less central to geopolitical history. That judgement is understandable but incomplete. His tenure asks a difficult question. What happens when a state that has recently discovered the uses of hard power is governed by a man who distrusts secrecy, nuclear display, and political centralisation? The answer is not failure. It is friction.

Desai’s morality was real. So were the world’s dangers. He believed India should not build nuclear weapons. Pakistan continued toward nuclear capability. He believed relations with neighbours could improve through sincerity. Zia-ul-Haq’s Pakistan remained strategically adversarial. He believed China could be engaged. China invaded Vietnam during the visit meant to reopen the relationship. He believed democratic restoration would strengthen India. His coalition proved too brittle to sustain itself.

The verdict of history must avoid both condescension and nostalgia. Desai was not merely naïve. He understood power but wished to discipline it through principle. He was not merely principled. His rigidity made compromise within his own coalition difficult. His foreign policy did not produce grand success, but neither did it produce disaster. It held India in a period between two storms.

By 1979, the Janata experiment was collapsing. Desai resigned. The promise of post-Emergency renewal had become factional exhaustion. The old questions returned with new urgency: Pakistan’s nuclear future, China’s inscrutability, Afghanistan’s instability, and the unfinished reckoning with Indira Gandhi’s political force.

For a brief moment, another elderly leader would occupy the prime minister’s chair. Charan Singh came from the soil rather than the seminar room, from the politics of the kisans rather than the diplomacy of chancelleries. But beyond India’s villages and Parliament’s intrigues, Soviet troops were preparing to enter Afghanistan. The subcontinent was about to be pulled once more into the machinery of great-power rivalry.

Chapter Five: Charan Singh: The Farmer at the Edge of Empire, 1979–1980

Chapter Five

Charan Singh: The Farmer at the Edge of Empire, 1979–1980

Winter came to Kabul before the world agreed on what to call the invasion. In the last days of December 1979, Soviet troops crossed into Afghanistan, aircraft moved over cold mountains, and a country that had long served as buffer, corridor, and graveyard was pulled violently into the centre of the Cold War. In Delhi, the government was weak, temporary, and consumed by arithmetic in Parliament. Yet beyond the churn of Indian politics, something immense had shifted. The superpower conflict had arrived at India’s north-western approaches.

Charan Singh was not made for the salons of grand strategy. He came from western Uttar Pradesh’s agrarian politics, from the anxieties of cultivators, land relations, credit, caste, and rural power. His political language drew from the village rather than the summit communiqué. Where Jawaharlal Nehru had imagined India in civilisational and internationalist terms, Charan Singh thought first of the peasant republic that he believed had been neglected by urban elites and industrial planners. He was a formidable political figure, but not a long-tenured prime minister. His government, dependent and unstable, lasted from July 1979 to January 1980 and never faced Parliament with durable authority.

A short-lived government faced the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan and revived U.S.–Pakistan strategic alignment. Charan Singh’s tenure was not long enough to generate a full doctrine. Yet history is not always courteous enough to wait for stable governments. Sometimes the event arrives before the state has arranged itself.

To understand the shock of Afghanistan in 1979, one must see what Afghanistan meant to India. It was not only a distant neighbour beyond Pakistan. It was strategic depth in reverse: a space that prevented Pakistan from becoming the sole gatekeeper of India’s continental imagination to the west and Central Asia. For much of the post-independence period, India had enjoyed cordial relations with Kabul. Afghanistan’s own dispute with Pakistan over Pashtunistan gave New Delhi and Kabul a certain convergence, though India handled it carefully. A friendly Afghanistan complicated Pakistan’s strategic geography. A hostile or militarised Afghanistan, especially one drawing Pakistan deeper into great-power patronage, could transform the region.

The Soviet invasion did exactly that.

For India, the fact of invasion created an immediate moral and diplomatic problem. A non-aligned country had been entered by a superpower with which India had close ties. Condemning Moscow outright would please the West and many non-aligned states, but would risk India’s Soviet relationship at a time when Pakistan, China, and the United States formed a potentially hostile strategic triangle. Refusing to condemn Moscow would damage India’s non-aligned credibility and expose New Delhi to charges of selective principle.

Charan Singh’s government stood inside this contradiction.

India’s response was cautious, uneasy, and widely criticised by those who believed non-alignment required a clearer defence of Afghan sovereignty. India did not celebrate the invasion as liberation. But neither did it join the loudest chorus of condemnation. Its position was shaped by realism, dependency, and fear of consequences.

In Parliament and policy circles, the concern soon widened beyond Afghanistan itself. The invasion gave the United States a reason to rebuild Pakistan’s strategic value. Pakistan under General Zia-ul-Haq became a frontline state in the anti-Soviet campaign. Arms, money, intelligence cooperation, and ideological mobilisation began to flow through Pakistani channels into the Afghan jihad.

This was the central consequence for India. Afghanistan’s tragedy became Pakistan’s opportunity. Zia’s regime, which had been under international criticism, especially after the execution of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, suddenly acquired strategic indispensability. The United States, which had been pressing non-proliferation concerns, now had a reason to look at Pakistan through another lens. China, too, opposed the Soviet presence. Saudi Arabia and other actors would become involved. The conflict would carry money, weapons, and militant networks into Pakistan’s security ecosystem. India could not yet know the full downstream consequences for Punjab, Kashmir, and regional militancy, but the architecture was being laid.

For Charan Singh, the problem was cruelly mismatched to his political resources. He had neither a stable majority nor the time to shape a durable Afghan policy. His government was interim in spirit even before it formally ended. The Janata experiment had splintered. Morarji Desai was gone. Indira Gandhi was waiting, reorganising, and preparing her return. Domestic politics reduced the room for foreign-policy innovation. The state continued, of course; diplomats wrote, officials assessed, intelligence officers watched. But the prime ministerial centre was weak.

The nation’s pulse in these months was not focused singularly on Afghanistan. India was still processing the collapse of the post-Emergency alternative. The promise of 1977 had frayed into factionalism. Voters who had removed authoritarianism now watched the opposition to it turn upon itself. Charan Singh’s constituency lay in rural distress and agrarian assertion, but the world beyond India’s fields was becoming more dangerous. There was a mismatch between the domestic argument and the external moment. In the countryside, questions of procurement, prices, land, caste, and credit mattered intensely. In the chancelleries, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Moscow, Washington, and Beijing mattered with equal urgency. A prime minister had to hold both; Charan Singh’s tenure barely gave him time to try.

The diplomatic corridor was crowded with uncomfortable possibilities. If India moved too close to Moscow’s position, it risked its moral claim as a non-aligned power. If it moved too far from Moscow, it risked weakening a relationship that had supplied diplomatic support, defence equipment, and strategic ballast since the 1960s. If it drew closer to Washington, it would find Washington moving closer still to Islamabad. If it denounced Pakistan’s new role too loudly, it could appear indifferent to Afghan resistance against occupation. If it stayed quiet, Pakistan’s military advantage might grow under the cover of anti-Soviet legitimacy.

Non-alignment, in this moment, was not a grand posture. It was damage control.

The economic ledger was indirect but real. The Soviet invasion did not immediately impose on India the kind of fiscal shock that a war on its own borders would have done. But Pakistan’s rearmament affected India’s long-term defence calculus. The more Pakistan became a conduit for American and allied support, the more India had to consider the future military balance. Defence planning is often a conversation with tomorrow’s adversary. The Afghan war lengthened that conversation and made it more expensive.

The security architecture did not undergo major reform under Charan Singh. The tenure was too short, the government too fragile. But the intelligence and diplomatic apparatus began watching a theatre that would haunt India for decades. Afghanistan would become not simply a foreign-policy issue but a source of militant movement, ideological hardening, narcotics flows, arms circulation, and Pakistan’s deepening security-state confidence. The consequences would not arrive all at once. They would seep.

This is why Charan Singh’s chapter, though brief, belongs in the architecture of the book. Some prime ministers command great events. Others are overtaken by them. Charan Singh was overtaken. His government did not create the Afghan crisis, nor could it resolve it. But it occupied the chair at the instant when the Cold War entered India’s neighbourhood in a new and violent form.

The verdict of history on his tenure is therefore modest but not empty. He represented a powerful strand of Indian democratic politics: the rural, anti-elitist, agrarian challenge to centralised developmentalism. In domestic history, that role is larger than his months as prime minister. In geopolitical history, his premiership was a bridge between the Janata interlude and Indira Gandhi’s return. The most important event of his tenure was one he could neither shape nor escape: Afghanistan.

A neutral assessment must acknowledge the limits of agency. To blame Charan Singh for the strategic consequences of the Soviet invasion would be absurd. To ignore his government’s cautious ambiguity would also be incomplete. India’s response reflected the accumulated dependencies of previous decades: Soviet defence ties, Pakistan’s adversarial posture, China’s alignment with Pakistan, and Washington’s willingness to treat South Asia through global rivalry. Charan Singh’s India did what weaker governments often do under great pressure. It avoided dramatic moves, preserved options, and left the reckoning to its successor.

By January 1980, Indira Gandhi returned to power. She came back not as the triumphant liberator of Bangladesh nor only as the defeated author of Emergency rule, but as a leader entering an altered region. Afghanistan was occupied. Pakistan was newly valuable to the United States. Zia was entrenched. Pakistan’s nuclear programme was advancing. Punjab was becoming troubled. Sri Lanka’s ethnic conflict was beginning to pull India toward dangerous involvement. The Himalayan frontier with China required management. Siachen, still a name known mainly to mountaineers and military planners, waited in ice.

The farmer-prime minister had passed briefly across the stage. The storm he witnessed would remain for the woman who followed him back into power.

Chapter Six: Indira Gandhi Returns: The Glacier, the Shrine, and the Cost of Command, 1980–1984

Chapter Six

Indira Gandhi Returns: The Glacier, the Shrine, and the Cost of Command, 1980–1984

At Siachen, the world narrowed to breath, ice, and altitude. Men moved as if underwater, every step breaking through crusted snow, every load heavier than it had been in the plains. The helicopter’s rotor beat against air too thin to trust. Below lay a white emptiness that did not look like a battlefield because battlefields, in memory, usually have roads, villages, trees, trenches, or mud. Here there was only the glacier, the Saltoro Ridge, and the knowledge that whoever climbed first would look down upon the other for years.

In June 1984, another place of silence would become a battlefield. The marble parikrama of the Golden Temple complex, the sacred pool, the sound of prayer, the presence of armed men, the movement of soldiers through a shrine: India’s state power and Sikh religious memory collided in a way that no later explanation could soften. Before that month was over, the republic would be altered. Before the year was over, Indira Gandhi would be dead.

Her second tenure began in January 1980, after the collapse of the Janata experiment. The voter who had punished her in 1977 now returned her to office, not necessarily in forgiveness, but in recognition that the alternative had failed to govern coherently. Indira Gandhi came back changed and unchanged. She remained centralising, wary, decisive, and often solitary in command. But the world around her had hardened. Afghanistan was under Soviet occupation. Pakistan had become vital to the United States. General Zia-ul-Haq’s regime was entrenched. Pakistan’s nuclear programme was advancing. Punjab was sliding into militancy. Sri Lanka’s ethnic conflict was beginning to pull India into dangerous proximity. China was no longer at war with India, but the boundary remained unsettled.

The first great pressure came from Afghanistan, though its heaviest consequences were felt through Pakistan. The Soviet invasion of December 1979 had revived Pakistan’s strategic importance to Washington. American military and economic aid flowed to Islamabad as Pakistan became the staging ground for support to the Afghan mujahideen. For India, this was not only a question of Cold War ideology. It changed the military balance in the subcontinent. It strengthened a Pakistani security establishment that India already viewed through the lenses of Kashmir, 1965, 1971, and nuclear suspicion.

Indira Gandhi’s public position was carefully balanced, sometimes awkwardly so. Indian statements often coupled the Soviet presence in Afghanistan with concern over U.S. military aid to Pakistan. This coupling was not rhetorical accident. It expressed India’s strategic anxiety: Afghanistan mattered, but Pakistan’s rearmament mattered more directly.

Her critics saw excessive indulgence toward Moscow. Her defenders saw realism. India depended on Soviet defence equipment and diplomatic support. It had not forgotten the U.S. tilt toward Pakistan during the Bangladesh crisis. China and Pakistan remained close. A loud Indian denunciation of the Soviet Union might satisfy moral consistency but weaken India’s security position. The old non-aligned dilemma returned in harsher form: how does a country preserve independence when each statement strengthens or weakens a relationship it cannot afford to lose?

Pakistan’s nuclear programme sharpened the danger. By the early 1980s, Indian strategic circles were increasingly concerned about Kahuta and Pakistan’s enrichment capabilities. Claims have persisted that India considered preventive military options against Pakistani nuclear facilities, and that Israel may have featured in some discussions or speculation. Public evidence remains too thin to treat such claims as settled fact. What can be said with confidence is that Indira Gandhi’s government monitored Pakistan’s programme closely and chose restraint. Preventive war against a nuclear facility would have risked escalation, international condemnation, and perhaps Chinese or American complications. It might also have failed to end the programme.

Restraint is rarely celebrated because nothing visibly happens. Yet in nuclear politics, the decision not to strike can be as consequential as the decision to strike. Indira Gandhi, remembered often for boldness, did not choose preventive attack. Whether this reflected prudence, lack of operational confidence, diplomatic pressure, uncertainty, or all of these, the result was that South Asia’s nuclear rivalry continued beneath the surface.

With China, her second tenure marked cautious reopening rather than dramatic reconciliation. India and China resumed official boundary talks in 1981, beginning a long process of managing disagreement without resolving it. These talks did not erase 1962. They did not produce a settlement. But they reopened a channel. After years in which the border had been a wound rather than a subject, dialogue itself became an instrument of stability. For Indira Gandhi, who had seen the political trauma of her father’s China policy, this was careful ground. The task was not to embrace Beijing but to reduce surprise.

The most immediate internal-external crisis, however, was Punjab.

Punjab’s troubles cannot be understood through a single cause. They grew from religious, linguistic, federal, agrarian, electoral, and security tensions; from Akali demands and Centre-state distrust; from factional politics; from the rise of Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale; from police failures and militant violence; from diaspora activism and alleged external support; from genuine grievances and manipulative politics. A history that chooses only one cause becomes polemic.

By the early 1980s, violence had intensified. The Golden Temple complex in Amritsar became a fortified centre for militants. The government’s White Paper on the Punjab Agitation presented the official argument that the situation had deteriorated into terrorism and that the state had exhausted alternatives. Parliamentary debate over that White Paper also revealed anguish and disagreement; members spoke of Sikh hurt, state action, and whether the army should remain near sacred precincts.

Operation Blue Star was launched in June 1984. The army entered the Golden Temple complex to remove militants. Militarily, the state achieved its immediate objective. Politically and emotionally, the consequences were devastating. The damage to the shrine complex, deaths inside a sacred space, and the timing around a major religious observance left many Sikhs feeling wounded and humiliated. The assassination of Indira Gandhi by two Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, followed by anti-Sikh violence in Delhi and elsewhere, turned crisis into national trauma.

A neutral account must walk carefully here. The state faced armed militancy and a breakdown of order. It also chose a military operation in a holy site whose symbolism could not be contained by operational success. Critics have argued that earlier political miscalculations, delayed decisions, and excessive central manipulation helped produce the crisis. Defenders of the operation have argued that the state could not permit an armed sanctuary to challenge sovereignty. Both positions appear in the historical record. Neither erases the human fact: after June 1984, trust between many Sikhs and the Indian state was gravely damaged.

In the middle of this, another frontier operation unfolded in near silence by comparison.

Operation Meghdoot began on 13 April 1984. Siachen had been an ambiguity on maps before it became a permanent military theatre. The Karachi Agreement of 1949 and later arrangements had described the ceasefire line up to point NJ9842 and then used the phrase “thence north to the glaciers”, leaving the high glacier region undefined. Mountaineering expeditions, cartographic interpretations, and military assessments gradually gave the area strategic significance. India moved first in April 1984, occupying key passes and heights along the Saltoro Ridge. Pakistan responded. The glacier became the world’s highest battlefield.

Operation Meghdoot was a tactical and strategic success for India. It placed Indian forces on commanding heights and denied Pakistan control of the Saltoro approaches. But it also created a long-term burden of extraordinary cost: lives lost not only to bullets but to altitude, avalanches, frostbite, crevasses, and weather. On Siachen, nature became the primary adversary and war the reason men remained there.

The contrast between Siachen and Punjab is instructive. In Siachen, the state acted pre-emptively in a remote theatre and gained lasting tactical advantage, though at high human and financial cost. In Punjab, the state acted internally after a political-security crisis had metastasised, achieving immediate military success but deepening a political wound. Both episodes reflected Indira Gandhi’s centralised style: decision concentrated, risk accepted, consequences large.

Sri Lanka formed the third gathering storm. The island’s ethnic conflict between the Sinhalese-dominated state and Tamil militant movements had direct implications for India, especially Tamil Nadu. Indira Gandhi’s government pressured Colombo and cultivated leverage with Tamil groups. Details of covert support remain contested and partly inaccessible, and therefore require caution. But India’s involvement was clear enough: New Delhi did not regard Sri Lanka’s Tamil question as a distant internal matter. Geography, ethnicity, refugee flows, and regional influence drew India in. The liabilities of this policy would fall heavily on Rajiv Gandhi after 1987.

The nation’s pulse during Indira Gandhi’s second tenure was restless and often fearful. The early hope that her return would restore stability gave way to a sense of accumulating crises. Punjab’s violence entered homes through newspapers and radio bulletins. Reports of killings, police action, extremist rhetoric, and political negotiation created an atmosphere of dread. In Delhi, the return of a powerful prime minister did not produce the emotional confidence of 1971. The aura was darker. Power seemed necessary and dangerous at once.

The economy carried its own pressures. Afghanistan and Pakistan’s rearmament increased defence anxieties. Siachen imposed high-altitude logistics costs that would become permanent. Punjab’s militancy disrupted one of India’s most prosperous agricultural states and imposed internal-security burdens. Sri Lankan involvement required covert and diplomatic resources. India’s development needs remained vast; every rupee committed to frontier posture or internal security came from a state still struggling with poverty, infrastructure, and industrial limits.

The security architecture of this period was marked by centralisation. RAW and the Intelligence Bureau were central to Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Punjab assessments. The army was used both at the frontier and internally. The air force became indispensable in sustaining Siachen. The police and paramilitary forces bore the daily burden of insurgency and order. Yet institutional coordination was uneven, and political oversight remained intensely concentrated in the prime ministerial centre.

The verdict of history on Indira Gandhi’s second tenure is harsher than on her first because triumph is less visible than damage. On Afghanistan, she preserved Soviet ties while warning against Pakistan’s rearmament. On Pakistan’s nuclear programme, she watched and restrained rather than launched preventive war. On China, she reopened dialogue. On Siachen, she approved a move that secured strategic heights and shaped the military geography of Kashmir for decades. On Sri Lanka, her government built leverage that later became entanglement. On Punjab, her government confronted militancy but left behind a wound whose pain exceeded the state’s operational vocabulary.

The human behind the power, in these years, appears less in speeches than in the solitude of command. Indira Gandhi had lived long enough in politics to distrust easy loyalty. She had seen adulation, defeat, imprisonment of opponents by her own government, electoral rejection, return, war, and conspiracy. By 1984, she was both formidable and besieged. In one of her last public speeches, delivered in Bhubaneswar on 30 October 1984, she said, “I am here today, I may not be here tomorrow.” The sentence has often been remembered because she was assassinated the next day. Its force lies not in prophecy but in the weariness of a leader who understood danger had come near.

On 31 October 1984, shots were fired in the garden of her residence. The prime minister fell. The republic entered one of its darkest passages.

Rajiv Gandhi, her son, was brought to power in grief and fire. Outside the hospitals and official residences, Delhi began to burn for its Sikh citizens. The next prime minister would inherit not only office, but blood on the streets, a wounded Punjab, a glacier war, a nuclearising Pakistan, a troubled Sri Lanka, and a question no son should have to answer in public life: how does one govern a nation while still standing at the edge of a mother’s death?

Chapter Seven: Rajiv Gandhi: The Pilot in the Fire, 1984–1989

Chapter Seven

Rajiv Gandhi: The Pilot in the Fire, 1984–1989

The smoke over Delhi in November 1984 did not rise like the smoke of war. It came from homes, shops, taxis, gurdwaras, and human fear. Men with voter lists and iron rods moved through neighbourhoods where Sikh families had lived for years among neighbours who now looked away, hid them, betrayed them, or were too frightened to intervene. A boy who had known the city as lanes and school routes learned it suddenly as hiding places. At Teen Murti and AIIMS and the prime minister’s residence, grief had the language of the state. In the streets, grief had become violence.

Rajiv Gandhi became prime minister in that fire.

He was forty years old, the youngest prime minister India had ever had. He had not been shaped by the long apprenticeship of the freedom movement. He had not spent decades in ideological struggle, prison, Parliament, or party organisation. He had been a commercial pilot, a reluctant entrant into politics after the death of his younger brother Sanjay, and then the son summoned by assassination to inherit a wounded state.

He received Blue Star’s fallout, Punjab militancy, Pakistan’s nuclear advances, the Sri Lankan Tamil crisis, Siachen, and China border tensions. To this must be added the anti-Sikh killings after Indira Gandhi’s assassination, a moral catastrophe that marked the beginning of his rule. Rajiv’s premiership would oscillate between youth and burden, technology and insurgency, reconciliation and coercion, diplomatic imagination and tragic miscalculation.

He won the December 1984 election with an enormous mandate. The scale of the victory reflected sympathy, hope, Congress organisation, opposition weakness, and the yearning for stability after violence. Rajiv spoke the language of a new India: computers, telecommunications, modern management, clean administration, decentralisation, youth, and efficiency. He seemed to some Indians like the country’s leap into the late twentieth century. Yet the crises he faced were older than his vocabulary. They were questions of land, identity, borders, language, religion, ethnicity, and state power.

Punjab was the first wound.

Rajiv Gandhi attempted political settlement through the Rajiv–Longowal Accord of July 1985, signed with Akali leader Harchand Singh Longowal. The aim was to separate Sikh political grievances from militancy and restore constitutional politics. The accord addressed issues including Chandigarh, river waters, compensation, army recruitment, and inquiry into violence. But implementation faltered, opposition emerged, and Longowal was assassinated within weeks. The agreement became an emblem of Rajiv’s early style: bold enough to reach for settlement, insufficiently anchored to survive the forces that opposed it.

In Assam, he moved toward a more successful compact. The Assam Accord was signed on 15 August 1985 after a six-year agitation over illegal migration, led principally by the All Assam Students’ Union and allied groups. The accord recognised the government’s anxiety to find a satisfactory solution to the “problem of foreigners” and set 24 March 1971 as a key cut-off date for detection and deletion of names of foreigners from electoral rolls. It sought to translate agitation into constitutional politics. It did not end all disputes over migration, citizenship, identity, and implementation; those would return repeatedly. But it did create a framework through which a mass movement entered electoral democracy.

Mizoram offered Rajiv Gandhi his strongest internal-security success. On 30 June 1986, the Government of India and the Mizo National Front signed the Mizoram Accord in New Delhi. Its terms enabled the end of a long insurgency and the MNF’s entry into constitutional politics. In the later history of Indian insurgency settlements, Mizoram stands apart because peace endured. Its success came not from military force alone but from political accommodation, amnesty, statehood arrangements, and the willingness of insurgent leadership to join democratic competition.

These three settlements — Punjab, Assam, Mizoram — show Rajiv at his most revealing. He wanted to cut through inherited stalemates. He was willing to sign. He sought solutions framed as modern, constitutional, and forward-looking. But the durability of such agreements depended on local legitimacy, patient implementation, and the management of groups outside the negotiating room. Mizoram had the conditions. Punjab did not. Assam remained contested but functional enough to redirect politics.

Then came Brasstacks.

In 1986–87, India conducted a massive military exercise near the Pakistan border, known as Operation Brasstacks. Pakistan interpreted the mobilisation as potentially threatening. India insisted it was an exercise. The crisis escalated into one of the most dangerous India-Pakistan confrontations before overt nuclearisation. General K. Sundarji, India’s army chief, was seen as architect of a more assertive operational posture. Pakistan responded with its own deployments. The shadow of nuclear capability, still ambiguous but increasingly present, hung over the crisis.

Rajiv Gandhi’s war room in this episode was not the dramatic space of declared war but the more ambiguous chamber of signalling. Exercises can train armies. They can also frighten adversaries. If the adversary sees rehearsal as preparation for attack, the distinction between exercise and mobilisation thins. Brasstacks ended through de-escalation and diplomacy, but it left behind important lessons about crisis management, military communication, and nuclear ambiguity.

The China frontier produced another test. In 1986–87, the Sumdorong Chu standoff near the McMahon Line brought Indian and Chinese forces into confrontation. India responded firmly, including through military deployments in Arunachal Pradesh. The crisis did not become war. Instead, it became one of the pressures that helped generate Rajiv Gandhi’s historic 1988 China visit.

From 19 to 23 December 1988, Rajiv Gandhi visited China, the first Indian prime minister to do so in thirty-four years. The visit led to the establishment of the India-China Joint Working Group on the boundary question. This was one of Rajiv’s most consequential diplomatic acts. It did not solve the border. It did not create trust. But it restored high-level political contact and built a process for managing the dispute. In the long arc from 1962 to the twenty-first-century LAC crises, the 1988 visit was an attempt to make disagreement administrable. Rajiv’s modernising instinct found foreign-policy expression here: create a mechanism, resume dialogue, keep the future open.

Sri Lanka would prove far less forgiving.

The island’s civil conflict drew India through geography, Tamil Nadu politics, refugee flows, strategic anxiety, and prior Indian involvement with Tamil militant groups. On 29 July 1987, Rajiv Gandhi and Sri Lankan President J. R. Jayewardene signed the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord in Colombo. The accord provided for cessation of hostilities, surrender of arms by militant groups, devolution through provincial councils, and Indian assistance in implementation.

The accord was meant to turn India from covert patron and regional pressure point into guarantor of peace. Instead, it placed Indian soldiers in an impossible space. The Indian Peace Keeping Force, initially deployed to oversee disarmament, soon found itself fighting the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam. The mission shifted from peacekeeping to counterinsurgency against a group that had once benefited from Indian sanctuary or sympathy in some quarters. Indian soldiers died in jungles and towns whose politics they did not control. Sri Lankan sovereignty, Tamil aspirations, Indian regional ambition, and LTTE ruthlessness collided.

Rajiv’s Sri Lanka decision remains one of the most contested of his tenure. Supporters argue that India had to prevent external powers from exploiting the island’s crisis, respond to Tamil suffering, and stabilise the neighbourhood. Critics argue that the accord was imposed without adequate consent from the LTTE, that India underestimated the military and political difficulty of coercive peace enforcement, and that domestic Tamil politics constrained exit. Both assessments find support in the record. What is beyond dispute is the consequence: the IPKF became a costly, unpopular mission, and Rajiv Gandhi’s own assassination in 1991 would be carried out by the LTTE after he had left office.

In November 1988, however, Rajiv’s regional-security doctrine produced a clean success. In the Maldives, mercenaries linked to a coup attempt threatened President Maumoon Abdul Gayoom’s government. India launched Operation Cactus, rapidly airlifting troops to Malé. The intervention succeeded, restoring the government and demonstrating India’s capacity for swift expeditionary action in the Indian Ocean. For India’s neighbours and external powers alike, Operation Cactus signalled that New Delhi could act as a security provider in its maritime neighbourhood.

The contrast with Sri Lanka was striking. In Maldives, the objective was narrow: defeat a coup and restore a recognised government. The operation was quick, legitimate in invitation, and politically uncomplicated compared with ethnic civil war. In Sri Lanka, the objective was broad, the conflict deep, the parties divided, and the legitimacy contested. Rajiv’s regional activism worked when the problem was discrete. It faltered when the problem was historical.

With Pakistan, Rajiv’s tenure combined confrontation, dialogue, and risk reduction. The India-Pakistan agreement prohibiting attacks on nuclear installations and facilities was signed on 31 December 1988. The agreement obliged both countries to refrain from attacking or assisting attacks on each other’s nuclear facilities. This was a durable confidence-building measure in a region moving toward overt nuclear rivalry.

The agreement’s importance lies partly in its modesty. It did not end hostility. It did not stop nuclear weapons development. It did not resolve Kashmir. But it created a taboo around attacking nuclear facilities. In South Asia, where suspicion often outruns communication, even narrow rules matter. Rajiv’s government, after Brasstacks and amid nuclear anxiety, recognised that adversaries required some shared restraints precisely because they did not trust each other.

The nation’s pulse during Rajiv’s years moved rapidly from sympathy to expectation to disillusion. The early aura of youth and modernity was powerful. He spoke of taking India into the twenty-first century. His government lowered the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen through the Sixty-first Constitutional Amendment, encouraged technology missions, expanded telecommunications, and projected administrative renewal. But corruption allegations, especially the Bofors scandal, damaged his credibility. Defence procurement became a political wound. The prime minister who had promised clean government became associated, fairly or unfairly in public perception, with secrecy and scandal. The Bofors controversy corroded the moral distinction on which much of his appeal had rested.

Security institutions during his tenure were stretched across multiple theatres. The army held Siachen, confronted China at Sumdorong Chu, exercised during Brasstacks, and fought in Sri Lanka. RAW and the Intelligence Bureau tracked Pakistan, Punjab, Sri Lanka, and insurgencies. The CRPF, BSF, and police forces dealt with internal unrest. Rajiv also inherited and advanced defence modernisation, but the political fallout from procurement controversies made future acquisition more cautious and contentious.

The economic ledger was mixed. Rajiv’s liberalising impulses did not amount to the 1991 transformation, but they loosened parts of the licence-permit regime, encouraged technology, and signalled a desire for a more modern economy. Yet security commitments were costly. The IPKF consumed resources and lives. Brasstacks imposed mobilisation costs. Siachen remained expensive. Punjab and Kashmir required internal-security expenditure. The state was trying to modernise while fighting yesterday’s wars and tomorrow’s insurgencies.

The verdict of history on Rajiv Gandhi is unusually unstable because his promise was so visible and his errors so consequential. He presided over one of India’s most successful insurgency settlements in Mizoram. He helped redirect Assam’s agitation into constitutional politics, though implementation disputes persisted. He attempted peace in Punjab but could not prevent violence from continuing. He reopened India-China relations at the highest level and helped create a boundary-management process. He authorised a swift and successful intervention in the Maldives. He signed a nuclear-risk reduction agreement with Pakistan. These are substantial achievements.

Against them stand Sri Lanka, Bofors, the incomplete handling of Punjab, the early failures of accountability after the anti-Sikh killings, and the widening of Kashmir militancy toward the end of his tenure. The Rubaiya Sayeed kidnapping and the full insurgency in Kashmir would fall under his successor, but the atmosphere had already darkened. Pakistan’s nuclear programme continued. The LTTE war against the IPKF became a grinding contradiction. A leader who had wanted to represent the future found himself trapped by the unresolved past.

The human behind the power remains poignant. Rajiv Gandhi did not begin life seeking the prime ministership. He entered it through family tragedy and national violence. His manner often seemed courteous, sometimes impatient, occasionally naïve, but not small. He could imagine large shifts. What he often lacked was the political machinery, historical patience, or suspicion necessary to manage the forces his decisions released.

By 1989, his majority was gone. The young prime minister had aged in office. The pilot who had once navigated instruments in the sky had discovered that states do not respond like aircraft. A control moved in Delhi might produce turbulence in Colombo, Amritsar, Beijing, Malé, Islamabad, or the jungles of Jaffna.

When he left office, the map he handed to V. P. Singh was alive with danger. The IPKF was still in Sri Lanka. Kashmir was close to eruption. Pakistan’s nuclear ambiguity had deepened. Punjab had not healed. Coalition politics had returned. The age of single-party dominance was giving way to something more brittle.

And in Srinagar, a young woman named Rubaiya Sayeed was about to be kidnapped, forcing the new government to choose between the life of a hostage and the authority of the state.

Chapter Eight: V. P. Singh: The Hostage and the Valley, 1989–1990

Chapter Eight

V. P. Singh: The Hostage and the Valley, 1989–1990

The snow had not yet taken Srinagar fully when the city began to change its sound. It was December 1989, and the Valley carried the tense quiet that comes before a crowd forms or a shot is fired. Shop shutters came down faster. Men lowered their voices near police posts. Posters appeared. Slogans moved from walls to streets. In one house, a family waited for a daughter who did not return. In Delhi, a new government, barely settled into office, was about to discover that the authority of the Indian state could be tested not by an army across a border, but by five armed men and a young woman held captive.

Vishwanath Pratap Singh became prime minister in December 1989 as the age of Congress dominance gave way to coalition arithmetic, moral protest, and fragmentation. His rise had been powered by the Bofors controversy, by his image as a crusader against corruption, and by a public appetite for accountability after the Rajiv Gandhi years. But the state he inherited was not waiting politely for reform. The Indian Peace Keeping Force was still entangled in Sri Lanka. Punjab remained troubled. Pakistan’s nuclear capability was moving from suspicion toward strategic reality. Kashmir was entering its most dangerous phase since 1947.

The first great test came within days.

On 8 December 1989, Rubaiya Sayeed, daughter of Union Home Minister Mufti Mohammad Sayeed, was kidnapped in Srinagar by militants associated with the Jammu and Kashmir Liberation Front. The demand was the release of jailed militants. For any government, a hostage crisis is a cruel compression of law, emotion, and power. For this government, the cruelty was sharper because the hostage was the home minister’s daughter and the crisis unfolded in a state already trembling. The militants understood theatre. They had seized not merely a person but the nerve of the new administration.

After days of pressure, the government released five militants in exchange for Rubaiya Sayeed’s freedom. The immediate result was relief. A young woman came home alive. The deeper result was more troubling. The state had conceded under militant coercion. In Kashmir, the release was read by many militants and sympathisers as proof that armed pressure worked. In Delhi, officials and security officers would later view the decision as a moment when the insurgency gained psychological momentum. Yet the moral difficulty should not be flattened. Governments are criticised for concession after hostages survive; they are condemned for hardness when hostages die. V. P. Singh’s government chose life in the moment and paid in authority thereafter.

Kashmir then moved with frightening speed.

The insurgency that had been gathering through the late 1980s entered open phase in 1990. Demonstrations, militant attacks, targeted killings, police collapse, fear, and counter-force reshaped daily life in the Valley. Jagmohan was appointed governor in January 1990. The state government under Farooq Abdullah resigned. Curfews, protests, firing incidents, and mass fear followed. For Kashmiri Muslims, the period brought militarisation, repression, loss, and uncertainty. For Kashmiri Pandits, it brought terror, targeted killings, threats, and flight. Families left in winter carrying keys to homes they believed they might soon return to. Many did not.

The Pandit exodus remains among the most painful and contested events in modern Indian history. There is no honest way to write it as a footnote. Militants killed prominent Pandits and others seen as aligned with the Indian state. Threats and fear spread far beyond those directly attacked. The atmosphere of intimidation, the collapse of confidence in protection, and the wider insurgent upsurge drove large numbers of Pandits from the Valley. At the same time, scholars and participants continue to debate the role of state failure, governor’s rule, militant strategy, local complicity, neighbourly protection in some cases, and later political memory. The human fact is not in doubt: a community was uprooted from its homeland.

For V. P. Singh, Kashmir was not a crisis that could be solved by one order. It was an accumulation: disputed sovereignty since 1947, electoral malpractice allegations, Pakistan’s support for militancy, local alienation, Afghan-war blowback, unemployment, radicalisation, policing failures, and the symbolic force of the Rubaiya concession. By 1990, the state faced not only armed militants but a legitimacy crisis in the Valley. Security action could contain violence; it could not by itself restore consent.

The war room in Delhi had changed from the days of 1965 and 1971. There were no armoured columns crossing Punjab, no formal declarations, no surrender ceremony on the horizon. Instead there were intelligence reports, funeral processions, protests, militant communiqués, diplomatic warnings, and the dread that Pakistan might escalate. The new grammar of conflict was internal-external: militancy inside Indian territory, support and sanctuary across the border, nuclear anxiety above it all.

The India-Pakistan crisis of 1990 brought these fears into the open. As violence surged in Kashmir, Indian and Pakistani forces moved into heightened readiness. Washington became alarmed. President George H. W. Bush sent Deputy National Security Adviser Robert Gates to the region in May 1990 to help reduce tensions. Later accounts have debated how close India and Pakistan came to nuclear war; some American assessments were deeply worried, while several South Asian scholars have argued that the crisis has sometimes been exaggerated in retrospective Western narratives.

That dispute matters. To say the subcontinent nearly crossed the nuclear threshold may overstate what leaders intended or forces were prepared to do. To dismiss the crisis entirely would understate the danger of ambiguity, mobilisation, and mistrust. Nuclear opacity can deter, but it also breeds fear because each side imagines the other’s hidden capabilities. In 1990, Kashmir, militancy, conventional military readiness, and nuclear uncertainty came together in a way that revealed the future. The subcontinent was entering an era in which even limited crises would carry nuclear shadows.

V. P. Singh’s government also ended India’s Sri Lankan military entanglement. The IPKF, deployed under Rajiv Gandhi after the 1987 Indo-Sri Lanka Accord, had become deeply unpopular in both Sri Lanka and India. Sri Lankan President Ranasinghe Premadasa wanted Indian troops out. The new Indian government committed to withdrawal. The last IPKF contingents left Sri Lanka in March 1990. The withdrawal was a strategic disengagement after failed intervention. It ended a mission that had begun as peacekeeping and become counterinsurgency. It saved India further losses and removed a major irritant in India-Sri Lanka relations. But it also left behind bitter lessons. Regional power could insert troops more easily than it could engineer political settlement. Tamil suffering, Sri Lankan sovereignty, LTTE militancy, Indian domestic politics, and great-power anxieties had proved too tangled for coercive peace.

The nation’s pulse under V. P. Singh was feverish. His premiership is often remembered domestically through the Mandal Commission decision and the upheaval over reservations for Other Backward Classes, as well as the growing Ram Janmabhoomi mobilisation. Those developments transformed Indian politics, but even in the geopolitical frame they mattered. A government consumed by social justice protests, caste mobilisation, religious mobilisation, and coalition instability had less political room to manage Kashmir and Pakistan. National-security decisions are never made in an empty chamber. The noise outside enters through every wall.

In Kashmir, the press carried images and reports that divided Indian opinion. Some saw only Pakistan-backed terrorism and demanded a hard response. Others saw local alienation and warned against reducing the crisis to external interference. Kashmiri Pandit voices carried grief and accusation. Kashmiri Muslim voices spoke of repression, anger, and betrayal. Human-rights concerns rose alongside demands for national unity. The state’s vocabulary narrowed to security at the very moment when politics was most urgently needed.

The ledger of V. P. Singh’s tenure is brief but severe. On Rubaiya Sayeed, the government saved a hostage but strengthened the perception that militancy could compel the state. On Kashmir, it faced the eruption of insurgency and the Pandit exodus but did not stabilise the Valley. On Pakistan, it passed through a dangerous crisis mediated in part by American diplomacy. On Sri Lanka, it withdrew the IPKF and ended a costly intervention. On national-security architecture, the security apparatus became reactive, stretched, and politically constrained. On the economy, internal-security costs rose, while the withdrawal from Sri Lanka reduced one overseas military burden. The balance-of-payments storm was already gathering and would break under his successor.

Historians have judged V. P. Singh primarily as a domestic transformer rather than a foreign-policy prime minister. Yet his national-security legacy cannot be dismissed. He governed at the hinge between two Indias: the single-party security state of earlier decades and the coalition-era state that would confront insurgency, terrorism, nuclear risk, and economic crisis simultaneously. He lacked time, cohesion, and perhaps the administrative grip needed for Kashmir. But he did not lack significance. Under him, Kashmir became the central internal-external conflict of the republic’s next generation.

The emotional arc of his tenure moves from moral promise to coercive exposure. He came to office as the man who had challenged corruption. Within days, the state conceded to kidnappers. He promised cleaner politics. He faced dirtier war. He inherited an intervention in Sri Lanka and ended it. He inherited Kashmir’s embers and watched them become fire. His government did not fall because of Kashmir alone; Indian politics had become too fractured for any single explanation. But Kashmir marked his premiership with a sorrow that outlived it.

When V. P. Singh left office in November 1990, the Valley was no longer merely disputed territory on diplomatic maps. It was a place of funerals, bunkers, slogans, migrations, and memory. The Pandit families who had fled waited for return. Kashmiri Muslims faced an increasingly militarised conflict. Pakistan sensed opportunity. India felt siege. The United States watched the nuclear shadow.

The next prime minister, Chandra Shekhar, would govern with even less parliamentary strength. He would inherit Kashmir, a Gulf war, and an economy close to collapse. In Delhi’s vaults, gold would soon become collateral. In the Gulf, Indian workers would need evacuation. And in the corridors of power, the republic would confront a humiliation different from military defeat: the possibility that it could no longer pay its way in the world.

Chapter Nine: Chandra Shekhar: Gold, Oil, and the Thin Edge of Sovereignty, 1990–1991

Chapter Nine

Chandra Shekhar: Gold, Oil, and the Thin Edge of Sovereignty, 1990–1991

At Palam airport, the metal did not look like a metaphor. It looked like cargo. Wooden crates, official seals, guarded movement, men speaking in low voices because everyone understood that some acts of state feel like confession. India’s gold, held in reserve by a civilisation that had long treated gold as ornament, inheritance, security, dowry, temple offering, and family insurance, was being prepared for movement abroad. The republic that had spoken for the decolonising world, fought wars, built dams, argued at the United Nations, and tested a nuclear device now needed foreign exchange badly enough to pledge treasure.

This was not defeat on a battlefield. It was humiliation in a ledger.

Chandra Shekhar became prime minister in November 1990 at the head of a fragile minority government supported from outside by the Congress. He had been a socialist, a parliamentarian of long standing, a rebel within the Congress system, and later a critic of centralised power. He had walked across India in the early 1980s, an act that belonged to an older moral tradition of Indian politics: the leader placing his body on the road to understand the country. But the office he entered gave him little room for pilgrimage or philosophy. His government had numbers only so long as others allowed it to have numbers. Outside Parliament, Kashmir was aflame. In West Asia, Saddam Hussein’s Iraq had invaded Kuwait. Oil prices had risen. Indian workers were stranded. Foreign exchange reserves were collapsing.

A prime minister can survive one emergency with authority, two with luck, three only if time and institutions cooperate. Chandra Shekhar had neither enough time nor enough political strength.

The Gulf crisis had begun before he took office, when Iraq invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990. More than 170,000 Indians were stranded in Kuwait and Iraq, many of them workers whose remittances formed part of India’s wider relationship with the Gulf. The evacuation that followed became one of the largest civilian airlifts in history. Although the main evacuation was initiated before Chandra Shekhar became prime minister, the Gulf crisis continued into his tenure. Operation Desert Storm began in January 1991. India had to manage diplomacy with Iraq, the United States, Arab states, and its own domestic opinion. Foreign Minister I. K. Gujral’s earlier meeting with Saddam Hussein had already become politically controversial, but it also reflected the practical necessity of extracting Indian citizens from danger. In crisis diplomacy, moral posture and consular duty rarely align cleanly.

Then came the refuelling controversy. During the Gulf War, India briefly allowed U.S. military aircraft to refuel on Indian soil. The decision provoked domestic criticism and was later reversed. For a country whose foreign-policy memory had been formed by non-alignment, anti-colonial suspicion, and opposition to foreign military entanglement, the sight — or even the reported fact — of American warplanes using Indian facilities carried symbolic charge. The government could argue that refuelling was limited and logistical. Critics could argue that India was compromising its non-aligned position in a U.S.-led war. Both arguments drew power from India’s own past.

The episode mattered because it exposed a transition not yet openly admitted. The Cold War was ending. The Soviet Union, India’s principal strategic partner for two decades, was weakening dramatically. The United States was demonstrating unmatched military power in the Gulf. India’s economy was in crisis. The old vocabulary of non-alignment still existed, but the world that had sustained it was disappearing.

Kashmir, meanwhile, did not pause for global realignment. The insurgency that had erupted under V. P. Singh continued. Militants attacked, security forces responded, civilians suffered, and Kashmiri Pandits remained displaced. Governor’s rule and counterinsurgency measures became central to the state’s presence. Pakistan’s role in supporting militancy remained a core Indian allegation. The Valley became a place where every act of security could deepen alienation and every act of political outreach could be read by some as weakness. Chandra Shekhar’s government had little political capital to spend there. It inherited containment without resolution and passed it on largely unchanged.

The most decisive crisis of his tenure, however, was economic.

India’s balance-of-payments position had deteriorated through the late 1980s. Fiscal deficits, external borrowing, oil-price shocks from the Gulf crisis, loss of remittances, political instability, and declining confidence converged. Foreign exchange reserves fell to levels insufficient to cover even a few weeks of imports. The crisis was not sudden in origin, but it became sudden in consequence. The government had to act under compulsion.

One measure became emblematic: the pledging of gold. The symbolism was devastating because gold in India is never only bullion. It is the bangle kept for a daughter, the coin in a temple, the chain sold in distress, the family’s last shield against misfortune. For the state to pledge gold was to admit that the national household had reached its own hour of distress. Economists could explain the transaction as rational collateralisation. Citizens heard something else: India had run out of room.

Chandra Shekhar’s war room was therefore not a bunker of generals but a room of economists, central bankers, finance officials, and political men counting reserves. Import cover, creditworthiness, debt obligations, IMF discussions, oil bills — these were the new battle maps. A country accustomed to thinking of sovereignty in flags and borders now had to confront sovereignty in payments. Could India decide freely if it could not pay for essential imports? Could strategic autonomy survive economic insolvency?

The answer that emerged after Chandra Shekhar would reshape India. But during his tenure, the crisis still felt like emergency rather than doctrine. Liberalisation had not yet acquired its later ideological narratives. It was not yet celebrated as reform or condemned as surrender in fully formed terms. It was compulsion. The old development model had reached a wall. Foreign exchange did not wait for parliamentary consensus.

The nation’s pulse was anxious and fragmented. In the cities, prices and shortages worried households. In political circles, the government’s survival was the constant question. In Kashmir, fear ruled daily life. In the Gulf, Indian families worried about relatives. In the business press and bureaucracy, the phrase “balance of payments” moved from technical vocabulary to national dread. India’s middle classes had not yet tasted the consumer transformations that liberalisation would later bring. They saw first the shame of crisis before they saw the promise of opening.

Chandra Shekhar’s political weakness deepened the economic panic. His minority government depended on Congress support. In March 1991, Congress withdrew support after allegations that the government had authorised surveillance on Rajiv Gandhi. Whether the episode was grave in itself or politically convenient, the result was fatal. The government fell. Elections were called. Then, in May 1991, Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated by the LTTE during the campaign, adding national shock to economic danger.

The ledger of Chandra Shekhar’s tenure is often treated as a prelude to Rao’s reforms, but preludes matter. On the Gulf War, his government navigated a difficult diplomatic environment and reversed refuelling permission under domestic pressure. On Kashmir, it continued containment without settlement. On the economy, it confronted the collapse of external confidence and began the emergency gold operation that revealed the severity of the crisis. On national-security architecture, there was no major reform; the state was too weak and the tenure too brief. On foreign policy, India stood between a fading Soviet order and an American-led moment it had not yet learned to engage confidently.

The verdict of history on Chandra Shekhar is constrained by brevity. He did not have time to leave a strategic doctrine. He did not command a parliamentary majority. He did not resolve Kashmir, redesign the economy, or reposition India in the post-Cold War world. Yet he occupied the office at the moment when the republic’s old assumptions visibly broke. The crisis under him made the reforms under Rao unavoidable. In that sense, his tenure was the last room before the door opened.

Neutrality requires acknowledging both his limitations and his circumstance. A stronger government might have managed the crisis more decisively. A government with more time might have framed reform more coherently. But any government in late 1990 and early 1991 would have faced brutal arithmetic. Oil prices had risen. Reserves had fallen. Political confidence was low. The Soviet Union was crumbling. Kashmir was burning. The Gulf was at war. Chandra Shekhar did not create these forces; he was trapped among them.

The emotional arc of his premiership is the movement from political defiance to economic exposure. He had spent much of his career resisting dominant formations and speaking in the language of principle. In office, principle met insolvency. The state that had often lectured the world on justice now had to persuade creditors of its solvency. It is one of history’s quieter cruelties that a leader associated with walking the soil of India should be remembered, in the geopolitical ledger, for the moment when Indian gold prepared to leave Indian soil.

When he departed, the election campaign had already become dangerous. Rajiv Gandhi’s assassination would alter the result and the mood of the country. P. V. Narasimha Rao, a man thought by many to be near retirement, would be called to lead. He would inherit a nation in grief, an economy near default, Kashmir in insurgency, the Soviet Union near collapse, and a world no longer arranged around the certainties India had known since independence.

The crates of gold had moved. The old order was moving too.

Chapter Ten: P. V. Narasimha Rao: The Silent Turn, 1991–1996

Chapter Ten

P. V. Narasimha Rao: The Silent Turn, 1991–1996

In the summer of 1991, Delhi felt like a capital waiting for bad news. Rajiv Gandhi had been assassinated during the election campaign. The Congress party, stunned and grieving, searched for a leader who could hold power without inflaming its factions. In the finance ministry, the arithmetic was pitiless. Foreign exchange reserves had thinned to a frightening margin. Gold had already been pledged, and more would be. In Kashmir, the insurgency was no longer a warning but a daily condition. Abroad, the Soviet Union — India’s old strategic pillar — was entering its final months. The Cold War world in which India had learned diplomacy was collapsing while India itself was close to default.

Pamulaparti Venkata Narasimha Rao was not the man many expected history to choose. He had nearly retired from politics. He was scholarly, multilingual, lawyerly, cautious, and often unreadable. He did not command crowds with the moral sweep of Nehru, the simplicity of Shastri, the will of Indira Gandhi, or the modernising brightness of Rajiv. His power lay elsewhere: in patience, ambiguity, silence, and a capacity to let events ripen until action became less visible than outcome.

He took office amid economic near-default, the Kashmir insurgency, post-Cold War uncertainty, the Soviet collapse, and terrorism after the 1993 Mumbai blasts. It was an inheritance that would have broken a more dramatic leader. Rao’s answer was not drama. It was repositioning.

The first theatre was economic, but it was also geopolitical. By June 1991, India’s balance-of-payments crisis had stripped away the illusion that strategic autonomy could survive without economic capacity. A country that could not finance imports could not speak with confidence about independence in world affairs. Rao appointed Manmohan Singh as finance minister, and together they launched reforms that dismantled major parts of the licence-permit regime, devalued the rupee, opened sectors to investment, reduced industrial controls, and negotiated with international financial institutions.

Manmohan Singh’s budget speech of 24 July 1991 became the public text of this turn. Quoting Victor Hugo, he said, “No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come,” and declared that the emergence of India as a major economic power was one such idea. The line has often been remembered as economic prophecy. In context, it was also an attempt to transform compulsion into confidence. India was not reforming because it had leisurely chosen modernity. It was reforming because insolvency had forced the door open.

Rao’s achievement was political camouflage of an economic revolution. He allowed Manmohan Singh to become the face of technocratic reform while he managed party resistance, parliamentary vulnerability, and ideological discomfort. The Congress had long spoken the language of socialism, public sector leadership, and state-directed development. Rao did not repudiate that inheritance theatrically. He bent it. Reform, in his hands, moved not as rupture but as adjustment — which may be why it survived.

The consequences for foreign policy were profound. Liberalisation changed India’s external imagination. Economic diplomacy acquired new weight. Relations with the United States, East Asia, Europe, Israel, and global financial institutions had to be reworked. India could no longer rely on the old Soviet-centred defence and diplomatic architecture. The Soviet Union formally dissolved in December 1991. Russia remained important, but the old certainties had gone. Rao had to conduct foreign policy in a world without the familiar geometry of two superpowers.

One of his boldest recalibrations came in January 1992, when India established full diplomatic relations with Israel. For decades, India had maintained a pro-Palestinian diplomatic posture while keeping limited contact with Israel. Rao did not abandon Palestine; India continued to support Palestinian aspirations. But he removed the artificial ceiling on relations with Israel.

The decision reflected post-Cold War realism. Israel possessed capabilities in defence, intelligence, agriculture, and technology that India valued. The United States, whose domestic politics included strong pro-Israel currents, was becoming more central to India’s external environment. West Asian politics were also changing after the Madrid peace process. Rao used the opening to diversify without loudly announcing ideological reversal. This was characteristic. He shifted India’s position while making the shift appear almost administrative.

China required another kind of patience. In September 1993, Rao visited Beijing and India and China signed the Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquility along the Line of Actual Control in the India-China Border Areas. The agreement committed both sides to settle the boundary question through peaceful and friendly consultations, maintain peace along the LAC, and reduce or limit military forces in conformity with mutual and equal security.

This was one of Rao’s most consequential strategic acts. If Rajiv Gandhi’s 1988 visit reopened the political door, Rao’s 1993 agreement began building the corridor inside it. The agreement allowed India to concentrate on economic reform and internal security without constant border escalation. In hindsight, after the Galwan crisis of 2020 and the militarisation of the LAC, the limitations of the 1993 framework are clear. Yet for its time, it was a major stabilising instrument. It bought India time.

Time was what Rao needed in Kashmir as well, though there time did not heal. The insurgency continued through his tenure with grim intensity. Militants targeted civilians, officials, security forces, and political workers. Pakistan’s support remained central to India’s assessment of the conflict. Indian counterinsurgency expanded, involving the army, paramilitary forces, police, intelligence agencies, and local auxiliaries. Elections were deferred until later in the decade. Human-rights allegations grew. Kashmiri Pandits remained displaced. The Valley’s political life was hollowed out by violence, boycott, fear, and coercion.

Rao’s approach mixed force, political delay, and international containment. He resisted external mediation on Kashmir and insisted that it remained a bilateral issue under the Simla framework. At the same time, India had to answer criticism abroad. The end of the Cold War had changed the human-rights environment. Western governments and NGOs scrutinised internal conflicts more closely. Pakistan sought to internationalise Kashmir. Rao’s diplomacy worked to prevent Kashmir from becoming a global intervention issue. His security apparatus worked to prevent the insurgency from breaking Indian control. Neither restored normalcy.

Then, on 6 December 1992, the Babri Masjid in Ayodhya was demolished by kar sevaks despite assurances and security arrangements. The act convulsed India. Riots followed in several parts of the country. Internationally, India’s secular image was damaged, especially in Muslim-majority countries. For Rao personally, Ayodhya became a defining controversy. Critics accused him of passivity, misjudgement, or worse. Defenders argued that he had been constrained by federal arrangements, assurances from the Uttar Pradesh government, and the dangers of premature central intervention. The dispute remains bitter because the demolition was both a domestic constitutional failure and an international reputational shock.

Its consequences did not stop with riots.

On 12 March 1993, Bombay was hit by a series of coordinated bomb blasts. Two hundred and fifty-seven people were killed and more than seven hundred injured, according to later court reporting on the case. The blasts marked a new kind of urban terrorism in India: serial, coordinated, spectacular, and linked to transnational criminal networks.

Bombay was then India’s financial capital and cultural engine. The blasts struck not merely people and buildings but confidence. The Stock Exchange, Air India building, hotels, markets, and crowded city spaces entered the geography of terror. The attacks were widely understood as retaliation connected to the communal violence after the Babri demolition, channelled through organised crime and cross-border support. This made the crisis both internal and external, communal and strategic, criminal and geopolitical.

Rao’s government responded through investigation, prosecutions, diplomatic pressure, and strengthened counterterror focus. Yet the deeper lesson was grim: domestic communal rupture could be exploited by external adversaries and criminal networks. The line between social peace and national security blurred. India’s secular constitutional order was no longer only a moral commitment; it was a strategic asset. When it weakened, the state became more vulnerable.

The nuclear file remained active beneath the surface. India resisted the emerging Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty framework when it appeared to lock in nuclear inequality. Rao preserved the nuclear option without testing publicly. Accounts have suggested that India came close to testing in 1995 but stepped back under American pressure after U.S. detection of preparations. Publicly, India maintained ambiguity. This ambiguity was not indecision alone. It was strategy: keep the option alive, avoid sanctions during economic recovery, resist discriminatory treaty pressure, and preserve room for a future decision. The overt tests would come under Atal Bihari Vajpayee in 1998, but the continuity from Rao’s stewardship was significant.

The war room under Rao was therefore often invisible. There were no large wars, no triumphant battlefield photographs, no surrender ceremonies. Instead there were currency files, IMF negotiations, Kashmir assessments, China communiqués, diplomatic openings to Israel, CTBT debates, terrorism dossiers, and coalition calculations. Rao’s governing style suited this invisible theatre. He allowed silence to do political work. He could appear passive when he was waiting, and passive when he was failing; distinguishing the two remains one of the challenges of writing him.

The nation’s pulse during his tenure was unsettled. The urban middle class began to sense economic opening: new products, new aspirations, a loosening of controls. Business communities saw possibility. Workers and socialists feared abandonment. Farmers watched reforms with mixed distance and anxiety. Kashmiris lived through militarisation and militancy. Muslims across India felt the wound of Ayodhya and the violence that followed. Bombay’s citizens lived through blasts that announced the arrival of a harsher urban-security age. The old Nehruvian state was not gone, but it no longer felt sufficient.

The economic ledger was transformative. Rao’s reforms did not solve poverty, inequality, infrastructure weakness, or bureaucratic obstruction. They did, however, change India’s growth trajectory and foreign-policy capacity. Over time, a stronger economy would allow greater defence expenditure, broader diplomacy, deeper technology partnerships, and more confident global engagement. The reform moment also carried costs: uneven benefits, social dislocation, and the beginning of new inequalities that would shape later politics. But from the standpoint of strategic history, 1991 marked the recovery of economic sovereignty through partial surrender of old economic controls.

The national-security architecture adapted unevenly. Counterinsurgency in Kashmir became more institutionalised. Intelligence coordination around terrorism gained urgency after Bombay. The foreign ministry recalibrated toward multiple centres of power. Defence planning adjusted to the loss of Soviet certainty. But large structural reforms in higher defence management remained limited. India was changing economically faster than its security institutions were changing administratively.

The verdict of history on Rao has grown more favourable with time, though not without serious reservations. Economists and many historians credit him, alongside Manmohan Singh, with rescuing India from crisis and initiating reforms that reshaped the country. Strategists credit him with managing the post-Cold War transition, opening relations with Israel, stabilising the China border through the 1993 agreement, and preserving nuclear options without triggering sanctions during vulnerability. Critics hold him accountable, in varying degrees, for the failure to prevent the Babri Masjid demolition, for the moral and political consequences of that failure, and for the harshness and incompleteness of Kashmir policy.

A neutral assessment must see the conjunction. Rao was neither merely the reformer who saved India nor merely the prime minister who failed at Ayodhya. He was both. He widened India’s external options while presiding over one of its deepest internal ruptures. He strengthened the economy while trust between communities frayed. He managed China while Kashmir burned. He established relations with Israel while maintaining the Palestinian position. He preserved nuclear ambiguity while global pressure rose. He turned without seeming to turn.

The human behind the power remains elusive because Rao cultivated opacity. Those who worked with him often described a man who listened more than he revealed, who could speak in several languages and answer in none, who understood that in politics silence could be shield, weapon, and confession. In a louder age, this made him appear cold. But the crises he faced rewarded neither sentiment nor flourish. They required endurance.

By 1996, Rao’s authority had ebbed. Corruption allegations, party decline, Ayodhya’s shadow, and electoral fatigue weakened him. The Congress lost power. Coalition politics returned in full. Atal Bihari Vajpayee would briefly become prime minister for thirteen days, a prelude to a later and larger role. The United Front governments of H. D. Deve Gowda and I. K. Gujral would inherit the post-Rao landscape: liberalising but unequal, nuclear but undeclared, wounded in Kashmir, cautious with China, and increasingly aware that India’s old strategic vocabulary had changed.

Rao left without the public affection granted to simpler heroes. His legacy worked like an underground river. Years later, when India spoke with greater economic confidence, when Israel became a visible partner, when China border agreements were invoked in crisis, when nuclear tests were justified as the completion of a long strategic arc, Rao’s shadow was there.

But in 1996, all that was still uncertain. The next prime minister would last only thirteen days. Sometimes history does not turn a page; it lets the page flutter, waiting for the hand that will hold it steady.

Chapter Eleven: Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s Thirteen Days: The Pause Before the Blast, 1996

Chapter Eleven

Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s Thirteen Days: The Pause Before the Blast, 1996

The prime minister’s chair had been reached, but not secured. In May 1996, Atal Bihari Vajpayee stood in Parliament with the composure of a man who knew the arithmetic had turned against him and the instinct of a poet who understood that defeat, properly spoken, could become memory. Around him sat a Lok Sabha fragmented by caste, region, ideology, ambition, and suspicion. The old Congress system had weakened. The Bharatiya Janata Party had emerged as the single largest party. Yet power in India no longer belonged simply to the party with the most seats. It belonged to the party that could persuade others to stand near it.

Vajpayee’s first tenure as prime minister lasted only from 16 May to 1 June 1996. In purely administrative terms, the tenure was too brief to reshape foreign policy, national security, or the economy. No war was fought. No treaty was signed. No institutional reform can be traced decisively to those thirteen days. The significance lay in continuity rather than substantive action: nuclear debate, Pakistan tensions, and coalition instability remained, but no major crisis was resolved.

Yet history is not made only by duration. Sometimes a brief tenure reveals the age more clearly than a long one.

Vajpayee had been in public life for decades before 1996. He had belonged to the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, served as external affairs minister in the Janata government, helped found the Bharatiya Janata Party, and developed a parliamentary reputation that crossed ideological lines. He was a nationalist, a Hindi orator, a poet, a party man, and an unusually successful coalition communicator. His politics emerged from the Hindu nationalist tradition, but his public manner often carried a conciliatory cadence that distinguished him from harder voices within his own political family. Admirers saw breadth. Critics saw moderation of tone without abandonment of ideological foundations. Both readings would shape later assessments of his prime ministership.

The India of 1996 was unsettled by transition. P. V. Narasimha Rao’s reforms had opened the economy but not yet delivered broad confidence. The Congress had lost authority amid corruption allegations, Ayodhya’s shadow, and fatigue. Kashmir remained violent. Pakistan’s nuclear programme continued. China relations were managed but unresolved. The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty negotiations were nearing conclusion internationally, and India faced pressure to accept a framework it regarded as discriminatory. The Soviet Union was gone. The United States was more powerful. Regional parties were rising. The voter had broken the old centre but had not yet built a new one.

The 1996 election produced a hung Parliament. The BJP emerged as the single largest party, and President Shankar Dayal Sharma invited Vajpayee to form a government. He accepted. The invitation was constitutionally proper but politically precarious. Other parties were unwilling to support the BJP, partly because of ideological opposition, especially after the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992, and partly because coalition politics was now being organised around anti-BJP and anti-Congress possibilities.

The war room in this chapter was not a military chamber. It was the parliamentary floor. Numbers were the battlefield. Letters of support were supply lines. The confidence motion became the campaign. Vajpayee had to persuade not only legislators but history that his party had arrived at the threshold of national power legitimately, even if it could not yet cross it fully.

His resignation speech has endured in political memory because it accepted defeat without appearing diminished. He said, in substance, that governments would come and go, parties would form and break, but the nation and democracy must endure. The speech’s force lay in its restraint. Vajpayee did not have the numbers. He therefore sought moral advantage in surrendering office before being defeated on the floor.

A prime minister who resigns after thirteen days leaves little policy record, but the emotional and institutional record matters. The moment normalised the BJP as a party of national executive claim. Until then, the BJP had grown as opposition, movement, and parliamentary force. In 1996, it entered the prime minister’s office, however briefly. The fact of entry changed political imagination. Allies who hesitated in 1996 would negotiate differently in 1998. Voters who saw a thirteen-day government could imagine a longer one. Opponents who had treated the BJP as permanently outside central power had to adjust.

The national-security ledger remained mostly inherited. India’s nuclear ambiguity continued. The CTBT debate intensified. Pakistan remained adversarial. Kashmir remained unsettled. Defence modernisation continued within existing constraints. The economy followed the post-1991 reform path without a new strategic turn during these days. No serious analyst can attribute later nuclear tests, Kargil policy, or coalition management directly to the thirteen-day government as policy action. But the tenure foreshadowed the questions Vajpayee would later face: could India declare itself a nuclear weapons power and still manage international fallout? Could it reach out to Pakistan while preparing for conflict? Could a BJP-led government hold a coalition wide enough to govern India? Could ideological inheritance coexist with diplomatic pragmatism?

The answer in 1996 was: not yet.

The nation’s pulse during those days was curious rather than convulsive. India had seen assassinations, wars, Emergency, insurgencies, riots, and economic crisis. A short-lived government did not frighten the public in the same way. Instead, it signalled the arrival of a new political grammar. Delhi’s drawing rooms, party offices, newspaper editorials, and regional capitals all understood that coalition politics had become permanent. The voter had not given a mandate for single-party dominance. The age of bargaining had begun.

The verdict of history on Vajpayee’s first tenure is therefore a verdict on transition. It was administratively slight but politically significant. It revealed the BJP’s rise, the limits of its acceptability in 1996, the decline of Congress hegemony, and the centrality of coalition arithmetic. It also preserved Vajpayee’s personal stature. He left office without the bitterness of procedural defeat and without giving opponents a spectacle of desperation.

In the human portrait, the thirteen days show Vajpayee at his most theatrical and disciplined. He had wanted power; no politician reaches that chair without wanting it. But he understood that clinging would damage the future he sought. The poet-politician turned resignation into rehearsal. The office slipped away, but not the claim.

After him came H. D. Deve Gowda, a regional leader elevated by coalition compromise, a man from Karnataka suddenly placed in charge of a republic negotiating nuclear pressure, Kashmir violence, Pakistan tensions, and China engagement. The prime minister’s chair had become less a throne than a rotating centre of gravity.

Vajpayee walked out in 1996 knowing, perhaps, that history had not finished with him. The nuclear file remained closed but warm. The border with Pakistan remained quiet but deceptive. In two years, he would return. In three, the heights of Kargil would be occupied under snow. And the thirteen-day pause would seem, in retrospect, like the held breath before India announced itself at Pokhran.

Chapter Twelve: H. D. Deve Gowda: Coalition India and the Nuclear Refusal, 1996–1997

Chapter Twelve

H. D. Deve Gowda: Coalition India and the Nuclear Refusal, 1996–1997

Rain changes the sound of Delhi. It softens the sandstone, darkens the neem trunks, and makes even the capital’s arguments seem briefly less metallic. In the monsoon of 1996, a man from Karnataka, better known for irrigation canals, farmers’ meetings, state politics, and the rhythms of Hassan than for the drawing rooms of Lutyens’ Delhi, found himself in the prime minister’s office. H. D. Deve Gowda did not arrive there through the old imperial avenue of national charisma. He arrived through arithmetic, compromise, suspicion of larger parties, and the new grammar of coalition India.

His ascent marked a deep alteration in Indian politics. The Congress had lost the commanding centrality that had once made prime ministers appear almost inevitable. The Bharatiya Janata Party had emerged as the single largest party but could not assemble a majority. Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s first government had lasted thirteen days. The United Front, a coalition of regional and left-supported forces, then formed the government with outside support from the Congress. Deve Gowda, until then chief minister of Karnataka, became prime minister on 1 June 1996 and served until 21 April 1997.

To many in Delhi, he seemed an improbable national leader. This was not simply prejudice, though there was plenty of that in the capital’s response to a southern regional politician whose English was not polished in the elite style. It was also uncertainty about the times. Could a coalition government, dependent on outside support, handle nuclear pressure, Kashmir, Pakistan, China, economic reform, and the post-Cold War realignment?

The great decision of Deve Gowda’s tenure was a refusal.

In 1996, the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty came before the world as the next great instrument of nuclear restraint. For many countries, it represented progress: a ban on nuclear explosions that would strengthen the non-proliferation regime. For India, the treaty appeared to freeze nuclear hierarchy. The five recognised nuclear-weapon states would retain their arsenals; countries outside that circle would be asked to renounce testing without a time-bound commitment to universal nuclear disarmament. India had long argued that nuclear restraint could not be legitimate if it protected the privileges of those already armed.

The public face of India’s resistance at the United Nations was Ambassador Arundhati Ghose. She argued that India supported genuine nuclear disarmament but would not accept a treaty that failed to meet India’s core concerns. Her most remembered formulation — that India would not sign the treaty “not now, not ever” — became part of the diplomatic folklore of the period. Because that phrase is often reported through later recollection and commentary rather than an official verbatim treaty record, it must be used with attribution rather than treated as a formal treaty text.

Deve Gowda’s government did not invent India’s nuclear position. It inherited a line running from Nehru’s disarmament advocacy through Indira Gandhi’s Pokhran-I, Rajiv Gandhi’s disarmament action plan, and Rao’s preservation of nuclear ambiguity. But the 1996 CTBT moment forced a coalition government to hold that line under pressure. It did. India blocked consensus in Geneva and refused to sign when the treaty moved to the UN General Assembly.

The decision was strategically significant because it preserved India’s option. Had India accepted the CTBT in 1996, the nuclear tests of May 1998 would have carried a different legal and diplomatic character. Deve Gowda’s refusal did not make the 1998 tests inevitable. It made them possible. That distinction matters. His government did not declare India a nuclear-weapons state; it kept India from being locked out of that choice.

The emotional architecture of this decision is easy to miss because there was no battlefield. The tension lay in committee rooms, cables, diplomatic drafts, and the pressure of being told that responsible nations should sign. The decision lay in saying no without a majority government of great strength. The consequence was preservation of strategic ambiguity. The reckoning would arrive later, when Vajpayee tested and the world imposed sanctions.

Pakistan remained the other nuclear shadow. Dialogue continued in limited forms, but Kashmir and terrorism blocked any real breakthrough. The insurgency in Jammu and Kashmir was still intense. Militancy, counterinsurgency, civilian fear, and displacement shaped the Valley’s daily life. Pakistan’s support for militant groups remained central to India’s case internationally. Yet Deve Gowda’s government lacked the political weight to attempt a dramatic Kashmir initiative. It maintained security containment and diplomatic insistence that Kashmir was a bilateral issue under the Simla framework.

Coalition governments are often accused of weakness in national security. The charge is sometimes fair, sometimes lazy. In Deve Gowda’s case, weakness did not mean surrender. It meant caution. The government did not have the authority to gamble. It continued Rao’s broad approach: contain Kashmir, resist internationalisation, keep Pakistan dialogue limited, preserve nuclear ambiguity, and avoid a large war.

China diplomacy continued along the path opened by Rajiv Gandhi and institutionalised by Rao. In November 1996, during Chinese President Jiang Zemin’s visit to India, the two countries signed the Agreement on Confidence-Building Measures in the Military Field along the Line of Actual Control. The agreement built upon the 1993 peace and tranquillity framework and addressed military restraint, force levels, exercises, air intrusions, and communication along the LAC. It was one of the foundational documents of the post-1962 border-management regime.

The importance of the 1996 agreement lies in its modesty. It did not settle the boundary. It did not define the LAC in a mutually accepted way. It did not erase suspicion. But it helped create habits of restraint. For a country still facing Kashmir insurgency and nuclear pressure, a managed China frontier was valuable. India needed time: for economic reform to deepen, for military modernisation to proceed, for post-Soviet defence supply arrangements to stabilise, for domestic politics to adjust to coalition rule. Border tranquillity was not peace, but it was usable space.

Deve Gowda’s background as a state leader shaped his political instincts. He was often described as “Mannina Maga”, son of the soil. Such labels can become sentimental, but they point to something real. He represented the arrival of regional India in the prime minister’s chair. His presence itself announced that national strategy would now be shaped not only by Delhi-born or Delhi-trained elites but by leaders whose legitimacy came from state-level politics.

This mattered for foreign policy in ways not always visible. Coalition India had to listen to states. Tamil Nadu shaped Sri Lanka policy. Punjab mattered in Pakistan calculations. Assam and the Northeast mattered in Bangladesh and Myanmar policy. Border states had views on trade, migration, insurgency, and infrastructure. The prime minister could no longer imagine national security as a conversation among only the Centre, the armed forces, and the foreign office. The federal republic was entering the room.

The nation’s pulse during Deve Gowda’s tenure was impatient and uncertain. The public had grown used to instability: Rao’s decline, Vajpayee’s thirteen days, the United Front’s rise, Congress support from outside, and constant speculation about survival. Economic reform continued, but without the dramatic clarity of 1991. The middle class wanted growth. Farmers wanted attention. Regional parties wanted respect. The Left wanted restraint in liberalisation. Business wanted predictability. Kashmir wanted an end to fear. The security establishment wanted continuity. Deve Gowda had to hold these demands with a government that could be undone by withdrawal of support.

The ledger of his tenure is therefore one of preservation rather than transformation. On the CTBT, India refused a treaty it viewed as discriminatory and preserved the nuclear option. On China, India signed a major confidence-building agreement along the LAC. On Pakistan, dialogue remained limited and constrained by Kashmir and terrorism. On Kashmir, the state continued containment without settlement. On economic policy, the post-1991 reform path continued, though without Rao’s crisis-driven momentum. On national-security architecture, there was continuity rather than structural reform.

The verdict of history has often been ungenerous to Deve Gowda because his premiership lacked the drama of war, reform crisis, or charismatic command. But this misses the quiet importance of coalition continuity. He did not reverse Rao’s reforms. He did not sign away India’s nuclear option. He did not allow the China frontier to deteriorate. He did not produce a breakthrough in Kashmir, but neither did he trigger a reckless escalation. In a fragile political moment, restraint was not nothing.

His limitations were also real. His government struggled to project authority. Congress support was conditional and unstable. The prime minister’s communication with the national public was uneven. Delhi’s bureaucracy and media often treated him with condescension, some of it rooted in class and linguistic prejudice, some in genuine concern over coalition drift. He did not leave behind a doctrine that bears his name. He did not remake institutions. He governed as a bridge.

But bridges are judged not by ornament. They are judged by whether they carry weight.

In April 1997, the Congress withdrew support, complaining of insufficient consultation and political discomfort with the government’s functioning. Deve Gowda resigned. Inder Kumar Gujral, his foreign minister, would succeed him and give his name to a doctrine of neighbourhood diplomacy. The United Front would continue, but the ground beneath it remained unstable.

When Deve Gowda left office, the nuclear file remained open. The CTBT had been refused. China was managed but not settled. Pakistan remained adversarial. Kashmir remained wounded. Coalition politics had survived one transition and prepared for another. The quiet man from Karnataka had not commanded history’s centre stage for long, but he had held the line at a moment when pressure came dressed as international consensus.

And in the stillness after his departure, India’s neighbours listened for a new tone. I. K. Gujral would soon offer one: generous, civil, almost courtly. But beneath the courtesy lay the same hard questions — Pakistan, Kashmir, nuclear ambiguity, and whether goodwill could survive contact with power.

Chapter Thirteen: I. K. Gujral: Courtesy as Strategy, 1997–1998

Chapter Thirteen

I. K. Gujral: Courtesy as Strategy, 1997–1998

The room in Malé was far from the usual theatre of India-Pakistan confrontation. No winter fog of Delhi, no barbed language from Islamabad, no troops visible beyond the windows. Only the ocean light of the Maldives, the managed civility of a regional summit, and two prime ministers meeting under the gaze of officials who knew how often hope had failed. Inder Kumar Gujral and Nawaz Sharif spoke in a moment when South Asia seemed, briefly, to have tired of its own hostility. Kashmir remained unresolved. Nuclear ambiguity remained dangerous. Militancy had not vanished. Yet for a short season, diplomacy recovered its manners.

Gujral became prime minister on 21 April 1997 and served until 19 March 1998. He was not a mass leader in the familiar mould. He had been a diplomat, parliamentarian, information and broadcasting minister, external affairs minister, and a man of letters with a refugee’s memory of Partition. Born in Jhelum in undivided Punjab in 1919, he belonged to that generation for whom India and Pakistan were not abstractions but severed geography. The border had cut through biography.

This mattered. Gujral’s foreign policy was not sentimental, but it did possess a civility shaped by loss. He knew that neighbours could not be wished away. He also knew that India’s size created suspicion. His tenure centred on South Asian diplomacy and coalition fragility, with the Gujral Doctrine, Pakistan dialogue, and CTBT-nuclear ambiguity as the principal themes.

The Gujral Doctrine had taken shape while he was external affairs minister under H. D. Deve Gowda and then continued under his own prime ministership. Its best-known principle was that India, as the largest country in South Asia, should offer non-reciprocal accommodation to smaller neighbours such as Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Maldives, and Sri Lanka where possible. It also stressed respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-interference, and the refusal to allow one’s territory to be used against another South Asian country.

In plain terms, Gujral was asking India to behave like a confident power rather than an insecure giant. Smaller neighbours often experienced India not as benevolent civilisation but as overwhelming proximity. Every Indian election, river project, refugee flow, intelligence move, trade rule, or security decision could become a matter of anxiety in Dhaka, Kathmandu, Colombo, Thimphu, or Malé. Gujral’s insight was that India did not always need exact reciprocity from smaller neighbours; it needed a neighbourhood less afraid of it.

This was courtesy as strategy.

The doctrine was not entirely new in substance. India had long mixed pressure and generosity in the region. But Gujral gave the instinct a name and a moral shape. He believed that India’s stature could not be separated from the quality of its relations with its neighbours. This was not charity. It was enlightened self-interest. A region of resentful neighbours would drain Indian diplomacy, invite external powers, and magnify every border dispute. A region of reassured neighbours might give India strategic space.

Bangladesh offered one important precedent. The thirty-year Ganga water-sharing treaty with Bangladesh was signed on 12 December 1996, when Gujral was external affairs minister in the Deve Gowda government. Water, as always in South Asia, was never merely water. It was livelihood, federal politics, seasonal anxiety, and sovereignty. The treaty showed that accommodation could produce durable diplomatic capital.

Pakistan was harder. The Gujral Doctrine did not apply to Pakistan in the same non-reciprocal way because Pakistan was not a small neighbour seeking reassurance from Indian dominance alone; it was a military and nuclearising adversary with a competing claim over Kashmir and a history of wars with India. Still, Gujral believed dialogue had to continue. In May 1997, at Malé, he and Nawaz Sharif agreed to structure talks between India and Pakistan. This became the basis of the Composite Dialogue process, a framework intended to address multiple baskets of issues including peace and security, Jammu and Kashmir, Siachen, Sir Creek, Wullar/Tulbul, terrorism and drug trafficking, economic cooperation, and people-to-people exchanges.

The Composite Dialogue was an attempt to solve a procedural problem that had repeatedly defeated substance. India wanted terrorism and cross-border violence addressed. Pakistan wanted Kashmir central. If either side insisted on its preferred issue as the gate through which all others must pass, dialogue stalled. A composite structure allowed both to place their concerns on the table without conceding hierarchy. It did not solve the disputes. But it gave diplomats rooms in which to keep speaking.

The war room in Gujral’s tenure was therefore diplomatic rather than military. Its instruments were not divisions or squadrons but agendas, joint statements, backchannels, and tone. Tone mattered more than hard realists sometimes admit. Between adversaries, language can either prepare publics for compromise or imprison leaders in accusation. Gujral’s language sought to soften the air.

Yet goodwill had limits. Kashmir remained violent. Pakistan’s military and intelligence establishment did not disappear behind Nawaz Sharif’s civilian overtures. India’s security agencies continued to see cross-border support for militancy as the central obstacle to trust. Gujral’s critics argued that generosity, without hard reciprocity, risked rewarding hostile behaviour or underestimating Pakistan’s security state. His defenders argued that refusing dialogue merely strengthened those in Pakistan who claimed India wanted permanent hostility. Both views reflected the old South Asian dilemma: if India talks, it may appear weak; if it refuses to talk, it may appear rigid; if it talks and violence continues, the dialogue itself becomes suspect.

Nuclear ambiguity sharpened the stakes. India had refused the CTBT under Deve Gowda. Gujral maintained India’s position. India would not accept a regime that froze nuclear inequality while leaving the established nuclear powers armed. Gujral did not conduct nuclear tests. He did not formally weaponise India’s status. But he did not sign away the option either.

This restraint was not indecision alone. India’s economy was still consolidating after 1991. Sanctions would have carried costs. Coalition politics limited room for dramatic action. At the same time, the strategic establishment remained unwilling to accept permanent exclusion from the nuclear weapons order. Gujral held the line without crossing the threshold. Vajpayee would cross it soon after.

The nation’s pulse during Gujral’s tenure was marked less by mass excitement than by coalition fatigue. The United Front government had survived the fall of Deve Gowda, but its dependence on Congress support made its life precarious. Lalu Prasad Yadav’s legal troubles in the fodder scam strained coalition politics. Regional parties bargained. The Congress watched. The BJP waited. Citizens saw another prime minister rise through compromise rather than direct national mandate. The office had become less majestic, more negotiated.

This was not necessarily a democratic weakness. It was the republic’s social diversity entering government formation more visibly. But it made foreign policy vulnerable to domestic interruption. Gujral could speak elegantly about the neighbourhood, but his government could fall over matters far from the foreign ministry.

The fall came through Sri Lanka’s shadow. The Jain Commission, investigating Rajiv Gandhi’s assassination, examined the role of the LTTE and alleged links or failures involving political actors. Congress demanded action against the DMK, a United Front constituent accused in the report’s interim findings of being soft toward the LTTE. Gujral refused to dismiss DMK ministers without fuller evidence. Congress withdrew support. The government fell. The irony was sharp: a coalition committed to federal pluralism was undone by the unresolved afterlife of India’s Sri Lankan entanglement.

The ledger of Gujral’s tenure is modest but distinct. On neighbourhood policy, he gave India’s regional diplomacy a named doctrine of non-reciprocal accommodation toward smaller neighbours. On Bangladesh, the water-sharing atmosphere benefited from that approach. On Pakistan, he helped launch the Composite Dialogue with Nawaz Sharif, creating a structured framework that later governments would suspend, revive, and reinterpret. On nuclear policy, he preserved India’s option by refusing CTBT constraints while avoiding overt tests. On security architecture, there was continuity rather than reform. On the economy, liberalisation continued without a major geopolitical shock.

The verdict of history has treated Gujral kindly in tone but cautiously in substance. Admirers see him as the gentleman-strategist who understood that India’s rise required neighbourhood reassurance. Critics see the doctrine as insufficiently attentive to hard security threats, especially from Pakistan-backed militancy and from smaller neighbours’ own domestic politics. Scholars of South Asian foreign policy often regard the Gujral Doctrine as an important conceptual intervention, even where its practical effects were uneven. Its enduring value lies in the question it posed: can India lead South Asia without frightening it?

The human portrait is unusually gentle for a prime ministerial chapter. Gujral had known displacement. Partition had taken his birthplace across a border. Perhaps that made him less eager to treat borders as mere lines of hostility. But he was not naïve about power. His doctrine recognised India’s asymmetry and sought to manage it. Where Indira Gandhi had often used dominance, Gujral tried reassurance. Where Rajiv had attempted activist intervention, Gujral preferred diplomatic accommodation. Where Rao had turned silently, Gujral spoke civilly.

Yet civility could not hold a government together. By March 1998, he was gone. The United Front experiment had exhausted itself. The BJP returned to power, this time with allies and a clearer path. Atal Bihari Vajpayee, who had resigned after thirteen days in 1996, would again take the oath.

Gujral left behind no war, no surrender, no dramatic reform, no grand crisis resolved. He left behind a tone, a doctrine, and an unfinished dialogue. In South Asia, these are not small things. But beyond the courtesy, the earth was shifting. Pakistan’s nuclear programme was ready. India’s nuclear option remained open. The CTBT had not bound New Delhi. Vajpayee would soon face the question Gujral had deferred.

In the Rajasthan desert, beneath the old testing ground at Pokhran, preparations were waiting for secrecy, decision, and a prime minister willing to turn ambiguity into declaration.

Chapter Fourteen: Atal Bihari Vajpayee: The Bomb, the Bus, and the Mountain, 1998–2004

Chapter Fourteen

Atal Bihari Vajpayee: The Bomb, the Bus, and the Mountain, 1998–2004

In the Rajasthan desert, secrecy had its own temperature. Men moved under ordinary skies while extraordinary work continued below the earth. Instruments were checked, shafts prepared, signals guarded, explanations rehearsed only in silence. Pokhran had known this before, in 1974, when India announced a “peaceful nuclear explosion” to a startled world. But May 1998 was different. This time there would be no elaborate ambiguity. The state that had refused the CTBT, preserved its option through decades of pressure, and spoken the language of disarmament while building capability was preparing to declare itself.

Atal Bihari Vajpayee returned to the prime minister’s office in March 1998, no longer the thirteen-day claimant of 1996 but the head of a BJP-led coalition. He was a paradox often described too easily: a nationalist with a conciliatory voice, a poet who authorised nuclear tests, a man who rode a bus to Lahore and then presided over the Kargil War, a prime minister capable of both dramatic gesture and deliberate restraint. His full tenure marked India’s passage into overt nuclear status and into the age of limited war under the nuclear shadow.

On 11 and 13 May 1998, India conducted a series of nuclear tests at Pokhran. Vajpayee announced that India had successfully carried out underground nuclear tests. The tests were justified by his government as necessary for national security in a difficult strategic environment. The world responded sharply. The United Nations Security Council passed Resolution 1172 on 6 June 1998, condemning the nuclear tests conducted by India and Pakistan and demanding that both countries refrain from further tests, stop weaponisation, and join the NPT and CTBT without delay.

Vajpayee’s decision had deep roots. China’s nuclear status, Pakistan’s programme, the perceived inequities of the global nuclear order, and India’s long technological investment all formed the background. Yet the political act belonged to his government. For supporters, Pokhran-II ended strategic hypocrisy and gave India a credible deterrent. For critics, it provoked sanctions, accelerated Pakistan’s reciprocal tests, and turned South Asia into an overt nuclear dyad. Both consequences followed. Pakistan tested later in May 1998 at Chagai. The subcontinent had entered a new age.

The war room after Pokhran was diplomatic as much as scientific. India had to manage sanctions, reassure domestic audiences, explain doctrine, and prevent isolation. Vajpayee wrote to world leaders, including U.S. President Bill Clinton, framing India’s security concerns in relation to China and Pakistan. The letter’s leakage angered Beijing and complicated diplomacy. India soon began a sustained dialogue with the United States, led on the Indian side by Jaswant Singh and on the American side by Strobe Talbott. These talks did not immediately remove all sanctions or resolve differences, but they began the process by which Washington and New Delhi would gradually move from estrangement toward strategic engagement.

Then, in February 1999, Vajpayee boarded a bus to Lahore.

The image was powerful because it was almost disarming. A prime minister who had tested nuclear weapons travelled by road to Pakistan, crossed the Wagah border, and met Nawaz Sharif. The Lahore Declaration of 21 February 1999 committed both countries to intensify efforts to resolve all issues, including Jammu and Kashmir, and to take steps to reduce the risk of accidental or unauthorised use of nuclear weapons. The associated memorandum of understanding addressed nuclear confidence-building measures and communication mechanisms.

The bus journey was not naïve theatre. It was diplomacy under nuclear conditions. Once both countries had tested, the need for rules increased. Vajpayee understood that overt nuclearisation could either freeze hostility into permanent terror or force a more careful search for stability. Lahore was an attempt to make the second possibility real.

But while the bus was remembered, the mountains were being prepared.

In the spring of 1999, Pakistani soldiers and irregulars occupied heights across the Line of Control in the Kargil sector. The intrusion came as a shock. The Kargil Review Committee later found overwhelming evidence that the Pakistani armed intrusion came as a “complete and total surprise” to the Indian government, army, intelligence agencies, and Jammu and Kashmir state agencies. It also noted that no agency or individual had clearly assessed beforehand the possibility of a large-scale intrusion across the Kargil heights.

The geography made the crisis brutal. Peaks and ridgelines dominated the Srinagar-Leh highway. Indian soldiers had to climb under observation and fire. Artillery became decisive. Air power was used carefully, with India choosing not to cross the Line of Control. That restraint was strategically important. India’s objective was to evict intruders while preserving international legitimacy and avoiding expansion into a wider war between nuclear-armed states.

The men who fought at Tololing, Tiger Hill, and other heights did so in conditions that stripped rhetoric to essentials: rock, snow, thin air, artillery, climbing ropes, and bodies exposed to enemy fire. Kargil became a soldiers’ war in national memory, but it was also a test of political discipline. Vajpayee’s government chose to fight hard within a limited frame. That choice helped India diplomatically. The United States pressed Pakistan to withdraw. Nawaz Sharif’s visit to Washington on 4 July 1999 became part of the diplomatic endgame. India reclaimed the heights at great cost.

Kargil’s outcome was Indian military and diplomatic success, but the victory carried a severe institutional indictment. Intelligence had failed. Surveillance had failed. Assumptions about winter vacating of posts had been exploited. Civil-military and inter-agency coordination required reform. The Kargil Review Committee became the basis for subsequent changes, including efforts toward better intelligence coordination, border management, and higher defence reforms. The victory therefore contained a reckoning: courage at the front had compensated for complacency behind it.

Then came Kandahar.

On 24 December 1999, Indian Airlines flight IC-814 was hijacked after taking off from Kathmandu. The aircraft eventually landed in Kandahar, Afghanistan, then under Taliban control. For days, India watched hostages through the terrible distance of television and negotiation. The hijackers demanded the release of militants. The government finally released Maulana Masood Azhar, Ahmed Omar Saeed Sheikh, and Mushtaq Ahmed Zargar in exchange for the hostages. The decision remains one of the most painful concessions in Indian counterterror history. Masood Azhar later founded Jaish-e-Mohammed, which would be linked to future attacks against India.

As with Rubaiya Sayeed a decade earlier, the state faced the hostage dilemma. Save lives now, or refuse and risk massacre. Vajpayee’s government chose release. Critics have argued that the crisis was mishandled from Amritsar onward, where the aircraft might perhaps have been immobilised. Defenders note the uncertainty, the danger to passengers, and the constraints of operating in Taliban-held territory. The moral arithmetic remains unbearable because there was no clean option. But strategically, Kandahar strengthened the perception that terrorist coercion could extract concessions from the Indian state.

The early 2000s brought another cycle of diplomacy and violence. In July 2001, Vajpayee invited Pakistan’s military ruler, General Pervez Musharraf, to Agra. Musharraf had been army chief during Kargil; he was now Pakistan’s president after the 1999 coup. The Agra Summit failed. The two sides could not agree on language around Kashmir and cross-border terrorism. The atmosphere had spectacle but not settlement.

On 13 December 2001, terrorists attacked the Indian Parliament. The symbolism was direct: the heart of Indian democracy under armed assault. India blamed Pakistan-based groups including Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammed. The attack triggered Operation Parakram, a massive mobilisation along the India-Pakistan border. For nearly ten months, the two nuclear-armed neighbours remained in a dangerous military standoff. The mobilisation imposed huge costs and raised questions about coercive diplomacy. India did not launch a full-scale war. Pakistan faced intense international pressure after 9/11, when the United States needed Pakistan in Afghanistan but also had to address terrorism concerns.

Operation Parakram’s verdict remains disputed. Some analysts argue that mobilisation coerced Pakistan into temporary restraints and signalled Indian resolve. Others argue that the slow mobilisation exposed India’s inability to create quick punitive options and imposed costs without decisive gains. The later debate over “Cold Start” and proactive operations emerged partly from this frustration. Vajpayee’s government had mobilised the army but stopped short of war. The unresolved question was whether restraint reflected prudence, pressure, or lack of usable military options.

In April 2003, Vajpayee made another peace gesture, speaking from Srinagar of extending a hand of friendship to Pakistan. Later that year, India and Pakistan agreed to a ceasefire along the Line of Control, which came into effect in November 2003. In January 2004, at Islamabad, Vajpayee and Musharraf issued a joint press statement. Musharraf reassured Vajpayee that he would not permit any territory under Pakistan’s control to be used to support terrorism in any manner; Vajpayee said dialogue could be sustained only if violence, hostility, and terrorism were prevented.

This was the closing turn of Vajpayee’s Pakistan policy: test, talk, fight, mobilise, talk again. It was not inconsistency. It was the rhythm of India-Pakistan relations under nuclear conditions. Military force could punish but not settle. Dialogue could open but not guarantee. Terror could destroy trust in an afternoon. Vajpayee’s statecraft lay in returning to the table after each rupture without pretending rupture had not occurred.

The nation’s pulse during these years moved from nuclear pride to wartime anxiety, from televised patriotism during Kargil to hostage anguish during Kandahar, from outrage after Parliament to fatigue during mobilisation, from suspicion of Pakistan to cautious hope after the ceasefire. The media landscape had changed. Kargil unfolded in the age of live television, creating a new intimacy between battlefield and living room. Soldiers became household names. Coffins returned to villages under cameras. National sentiment was amplified, personalised, and broadcast.

The economic ledger was mixed but ultimately favourable compared with the crisis years. Pokhran triggered sanctions, but India weathered them. The economy continued to liberalise, information technology grew, foreign exchange strengthened, and India’s global economic confidence increased. Kargil and Parakram imposed major military costs. Defence procurement and border surveillance gained urgency. The Golden Quadrilateral and infrastructure push under Vajpayee also carried strategic significance: roads were not only development, they were state capacity.

The national-security architecture changed after Kargil. The review process led to reforms in intelligence coordination, border management, and higher defence structures, including the creation of the Defence Intelligence Agency and Integrated Defence Staff in the early 2000s. The National Security Council system gained greater salience. Yet some recommendations, especially around a Chief of Defence Staff, remained unrealised until much later. Vajpayee’s period thus began the reform but did not complete it.

The verdict of history on Vajpayee’s full tenure is unusually rich because it contains contradiction without incoherence. He declared India a nuclear weapons power but pursued nuclear confidence-building. He sought peace in Lahore but fought Kargil. He released terrorists at Kandahar but later mobilised after Parliament. He invited Musharraf to Agra after Kargil and then reached the 2004 Islamabad understanding after Parakram. His critics see dangerous symbolism, intelligence failures, and concessions under pressure. His defenders see strategic clarity, restraint under nuclear risk, diplomatic maturity, and the consolidation of India’s global position.

A neutral account must hold both. Pokhran-II gave India overt deterrent status but triggered Pakistan’s reciprocal tests. Lahore was courageous but vulnerable to Pakistan’s military establishment. Kargil was a battlefield success but an intelligence failure. Kandahar saved hostages but released men who would haunt India’s security. Parakram showed resolve but exposed mobilisation limits. The 2003 ceasefire and 2004 statement created one of the more stable openings in India-Pakistan relations after 1998.

The human behind the power appears most clearly in Vajpayee’s pauses. His speeches often moved between firmness and melancholy, as if he understood that nationalism without sorrow becomes dangerous. He could speak of war and peace without making either sound easy. That did not absolve his government of errors. It did make his political persona distinct: a leader willing to test bombs and board buses, to order force and write poetry, to absorb betrayal and still reopen dialogue.

In May 2004, the voters removed his government despite the “India Shining” campaign. Economic confidence had not translated evenly into electoral satisfaction. Rural distress, coalition dynamics, and anti-incumbency mattered. Vajpayee left office having transformed India’s strategic posture but without resolving its central disputes.

Manmohan Singh would inherit nuclear India, the Lahore-Kargil-Parakram legacy, a tentative Pakistan opening, a rising China, post-9/11 geopolitics, and an economy ready for faster growth. Vajpayee had made India’s power more visible. Singh would try to make that power acceptable to the world. But in Mumbai, trains and hotels and a crowded city still waited unknowingly for the next grammar of terror.

Chapter Fifteen: Manmohan Singh: Restraint in the Age of Terror, 2004–2014

Chapter Fifteen

Manmohan Singh: Restraint in the Age of Terror, 2004–2014

At Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, the city did not at first understand that war had entered through the ticket hall. Mumbai was used to movement — commuters pushing through evening crowds, vendors calling over the metallic groan of trains, announcements dissolving into static, families waiting under high arches built by an empire that had gone. Then came gunfire. Bodies fell beside luggage. A child cried. Mobile phones rang unanswered. Across the city, at the Taj, the Oberoi, Nariman House, Cama Hospital, and Leopold Café, ten men with rifles and grenades turned India’s financial capital into a theatre of televised siege.

In Delhi, Manmohan Singh faced the question that would define the moral and strategic centre of his premiership: how does a state answer spectacular terror when the answer expected by public rage may also be the answer desired by the terrorists?

Singh had entered office in May 2004 as an unusual prime minister. He was not a dynastic inheritor, mass agitator, wartime figure, or coalition street-fighter. He was an economist, former finance minister, technocrat, and public servant whose reputation rested on the 1991 reforms. Sonia Gandhi’s refusal of the prime ministership had placed him at the head of the United Progressive Alliance government. Critics would later call him constrained by party leadership; admirers would call him dignified, scholarly, and understated. The office he held was powerful. The political structure around him was complicated.

He received nuclear India, Vajpayee’s tentative Pakistan opening, a rising China, post-9/11 American strategy, and the opportunities of high-growth economic diplomacy. He governed a country whose economy was expanding, whose global profile was rising, and whose security problems remained brutally old: Pakistan-backed terrorism, Kashmir, China’s boundary pressure, insurgencies, maritime vulnerability, and neighbourhood instability.

His first great wager was peace with Pakistan.

Vajpayee had left behind the 2003 Line of Control ceasefire and the January 2004 Islamabad statement, in which Pakistan had committed not to permit territory under its control to be used to support terrorism. Singh continued the Composite Dialogue and encouraged backchannel discussions on Kashmir. Cross-LoC bus services, trade, people-to-people contacts, and humanitarian measures suggested that a frozen conflict could be softened even if not formally settled. For a time, there was movement. The ceasefire held better than many expected. The atmosphere was not warm, but it was less poisonous.

The backchannel, associated with Indian and Pakistani interlocutors including Satinder Lambah and Tariq Aziz, reportedly explored ideas around making borders irrelevant, self-governance, demilitarisation calibrated to conditions, and joint mechanisms. Because the exact terms were never formally adopted and much of the record remains memoir-based or journalistic, care is required. But the broad direction is clear: Singh was willing to think beyond slogans while staying within India’s formal position that borders could not be redrawn. His temperament suited this work. He preferred quiet architecture to public spectacle.

The diplomatic corridor widened dramatically with the United States. On 18 July 2005, Singh and President George W. Bush issued a joint statement in Washington declaring their resolve to transform the relationship and establish a global partnership. The statement recorded that Bush would work to achieve full civil nuclear energy cooperation with India and that Singh conveyed India’s willingness to identify and separate civilian and military nuclear facilities and place civilian facilities under International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards.

This was a strategic breakthrough of the first order. For decades after Pokhran-I, India had lived under nuclear restrictions. After Pokhran-II, sanctions had reinforced isolation, though they gradually eased. The civil nuclear agreement sought to reconcile India’s status as a nuclear-armed state outside the NPT with access to global nuclear commerce. India would not sign the NPT as a non-nuclear-weapon state. The United States would help open the international nuclear order to India. In effect, Washington was recognising India as an exception.

The political battle at home was fierce. Critics on the Left warned against strategic subordination to the United States. Some strategic conservatives worried about constraints on India’s nuclear weapons programme. Supporters argued that the deal ended nuclear isolation, strengthened energy security, and signalled India’s arrival as a major power. Singh staked his government’s survival on it. In July 2008, after the Left withdrew support, his government faced and won a confidence vote. The victory allowed the agreement to proceed through the IAEA and Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver process. Few episodes better show Singh’s hidden steel. The quiet prime minister risked office for a strategic bargain he believed would alter India’s future.

Yet while India was integrating with global power, terror kept returning to its cities.

On 11 July 2006, serial blasts on Mumbai’s suburban trains killed more than 180 people. The attacks struck the daily machinery of working Mumbai: first-class compartments, evening rush, commuters heading home. India blamed Pakistan-based elements; Pakistan denied involvement. The peace process paused but did not die. Singh resumed engagement later, arguing that dialogue and counterterror pressure could coexist. To critics, this looked like softness. To supporters, it reflected strategic discipline: India should not allow terrorists to determine its diplomatic calendar. The dispute would return with greater force after 26/11.

The 2008 Mumbai attacks were different in scale, duration, and media effect. They unfolded live for nearly three days, producing an intimacy of fear that earlier attacks had not. The captured gunman, Ajmal Kasab, provided direct evidence of Pakistani origin. India identified Lashkar-e-Taiba and Pakistan-based handlers as central to the operation. In the Lok Sabha on 11 December 2008, Singh said the attacks were carried out by a group that came from Pakistan and that India expected Pakistan to prevent the use of its territory for terrorism against India.

The public demand for retaliation was intense. Mumbai’s anger was not theatrical. Citizens had watched commandos fight room to room, hotel fires burn, hostages die, and police officers fall. There was fury at Pakistan, but also at Indian state failure: coastal gaps, intelligence lapses, poor police equipment, confused response, and the spectacle of a city abandoned to improvisation.

Singh chose not to launch military strikes. His government pursued diplomatic coercion, intelligence cooperation with international partners, dossiers against Pakistan-based actors, and pressure through Washington and other capitals. Pakistan came under pressure to act against Lashkar-e-Taiba figures, though India remained dissatisfied with the pace and sincerity of Pakistani prosecutions. The strategic logic of restraint was that a military strike could escalate under nuclear conditions, unify Pakistan’s domestic opinion, and distract from the global isolation of Pakistan-based terror networks. The political cost was that many Indians saw restraint as weakness.

This is the central tension of Singh’s decade. He believed, or acted as if he believed, that India’s rise depended on denying terrorists the power to derail economic growth and diplomatic integration. His critics believed that without visible punitive force, deterrence eroded. Neither side can be dismissed. The absence of war after 26/11 avoided unpredictable escalation. It also contributed to a later Indian debate over whether restraint had become strategically unsustainable.

The institutional response was significant. Parliament enacted the National Investigation Agency Act, 2008, creating a central counterterror investigation body for scheduled offences with national and cross-border implications. Coastal security was strengthened, maritime surveillance improved, and intelligence coordination mechanisms were revised. These reforms did not erase the memory of failure, but they marked recognition that India’s internal-security architecture had been overtaken by the threat.

The Sharm el-Sheikh episode in 2009 revealed the risks of Singh’s diplomatic persistence. Meeting Pakistani Prime Minister Yousaf Raza Gilani in Egypt on 16 July 2009, Singh agreed to a joint statement that said action on terrorism should not be linked to the Composite Dialogue process and included a Pakistani reference to threats in Balochistan. In India, the Balochistan reference caused a storm. Critics argued that India had allowed Pakistan to imply equivalence between Pakistan-backed terrorism against India and alleged Indian interference in Balochistan. Singh defended engagement, but the backlash narrowed his room. Sharm el-Sheikh showed that diplomatic creativity after terror required domestic trust, and that trust had thinned. The prime minister who had survived the nuclear-deal confidence vote could not easily carry the country on Pakistan after Mumbai.

China presented a quieter but steadily growing challenge. Trade expanded rapidly, but mistrust deepened. The boundary remained unsettled. China’s position on Arunachal Pradesh hardened in rhetoric. Stapled visas for residents of Jammu and Kashmir and Arunachal Pradesh became points of contention. Border incidents and patrol face-offs increased. The Depsang standoff of 2013, when Chinese troops pitched tents in an area India claimed in Ladakh, exposed the fragility of the border-management agreements built since 1993. Singh’s government avoided escalation and restored status quo through negotiations, but the episode foreshadowed a harsher LAC era.

The Singh years also witnessed the final phase of Sri Lanka’s civil war. In 2008–09, the Sri Lankan military crushed the LTTE. India supported Sri Lanka’s territorial integrity and had no desire to see the LTTE revived, especially after Rajiv Gandhi’s assassination. At the same time, Tamil civilian suffering created political pressure in Tamil Nadu and humanitarian concern in New Delhi. Singh’s government pressed for civilian protection and relief but did not intervene to stop Colombo’s final offensive. The outcome was restrained diplomacy with limited humanitarian leverage. Strategically, the LTTE’s defeat removed one militant actor. Morally and politically, allegations of mass civilian casualties and wartime abuses continued to haunt Sri Lanka and India’s regional image.

The nation’s pulse during Singh’s tenure divided between aspiration and insecurity. The economy grew rapidly in the first UPA term. Urban India felt the expansion of consumption, services, real estate, telecom, aviation, and global opportunity. The civil nuclear deal fed the sense that India was being admitted into higher councils. Yet terror attacks punctured confidence. Farmers’ distress persisted. Maoist violence grew in central India, leading Singh to call Left-wing extremism one of India’s gravest internal-security challenges. Corruption scandals in the second term — 2G, Commonwealth Games, coal allocation — corroded the government’s authority. By 2011–12, the public mood had shifted from admiration of growth to anger at governance failure.

The ledger is substantial. On Pakistan, Singh pursued perhaps the most serious post-Partition backchannel effort on Kashmir, maintained dialogue after attacks when he believed conditions allowed, but failed to secure a final settlement or lasting counterterror assurances. On the United States, he achieved a civil nuclear breakthrough that ended much of India’s nuclear isolation and deepened strategic convergence. On terrorism, he chose restraint after 26/11 and built institutional reforms, including the NIA and coastal-security improvements, but faced criticism for not imposing visible costs on Pakistan. On China, he managed frictions without war but did not arrest the deterioration of trust. On Sri Lanka, he balanced anti-LTTE security interests with humanitarian concern, but India’s leverage proved limited. On the economy, growth gave India global confidence, while the 2008 global financial crisis and later corruption scandals weakened the second term.

The verdict of history on Manmohan Singh remains contested. Admirers see a prime minister who understood that economic strength was strategic strength, who brought India closer to the United States without abandoning Russia or the developing world, who kept Pakistan dialogue alive under impossible conditions, and who resisted war fever after Mumbai. Critics see a leader too restrained after terror, too dependent on party leadership, too slow on corruption, and unable to respond decisively to China’s incremental pressure. Both portraits draw from the record.

The human behind the power was never theatrical. Singh’s silences became a political liability, but they were also part of his character. He spoke softly even when taking large risks. The nuclear deal showed that he could be stubborn beneath courtesy. After 26/11, his restraint was not passivity in the simple sense; it was a decision made under the knowledge that any military response against Pakistan could become something larger than intended. Whether that decision deterred future attacks or encouraged adversaries remains disputed. What is not disputed is the burden of making it while Mumbai still smouldered.

By 2014, the mood had turned. The electorate wanted decisiveness, visibility, and a state that appeared less apologetic about power. Narendra Modi campaigned on governance, development, national pride, and strength. Singh left office with India more integrated into the world than he had found it, but also with the public convinced that integration had not been matched by sufficient authority.

The next prime minister would inherit the institutions Singh strengthened and the frustrations he could not answer. The questions after Mumbai did not disappear. They waited at Pathankot, Uri, Pulwama, Balakot, Doklam, Galwan, Ottawa, Washington, Malé, the Red Sea, and the Line of Actual Control. India’s rise had made it more visible. Visibility would bring power. It would also bring exposure.

Chapter Sixteen: Narendra Modi: The Line, the Strike, and the New Weight of Power, 2014–Present

Chapter Sixteen

Narendra Modi: The Line, the Strike, and the New Weight of Power, 2014–Present

At Galwan, night did not soften the mountains. It hardened them. The river moved black between stone walls, the air thin and metallic, the ground uneven beneath boots already strained by weeks of forward deployment. Men faced one another in darkness with clubs, rods, stones, fists, and the accumulated suspicion of two civilisations armed with nuclear weapons. There were no gunshots. That made the violence more intimate. In June 2020, on a high Himalayan frontier where India and China had spent decades trying to manage disagreement without bloodshed, soldiers died again.

Narendra Modi had come to power six years earlier with a different image: rallies, development, decisiveness, Gujarat’s administrative reputation, and the promise of a state that would move faster and strike harder. His mandate in 2014 was the first single-party majority in India since 1984. In 2019, he returned with an even larger majority. In 2024, he began a third term, this time dependent on allies in a way his first two terms had not required. The strategic environment of his tenure included Pakistan-backed terrorism concerns, China’s rising assertiveness, U.S.–India convergence, economic nationalism, a militarised LAC, Quad consolidation, Russia–Ukraine balancing, West Asia crises, diplomatic rows with Canada and the United States, and later the 2025 India–Pakistan crisis.

Modi’s foreign and security policy has been defined by visible power. Earlier prime ministers had used force across borders, sometimes covertly, sometimes openly. Modi’s difference lay partly in publicity, partly in doctrine, and partly in political communication. Retaliation became not merely an instrument of statecraft but a public signal: India would no longer absorb attacks without imposing costs. Critics questioned the durability and risks of this posture. Supporters saw overdue correction.

The first signs came in the east. In 2015, after militants attacked Indian soldiers in Manipur, India said its forces had struck insurgent groups along the Myanmar border. Details remain contested, and Myanmar’s role and the exact geography of the operation were debated. The episode mattered less for battlefield scale than for signalling: cross-border counterinsurgency would be publicly claimed as evidence of a more assertive state.

Then came Pathankot.

In January 2016, terrorists attacked the Pathankot airbase. India blamed Jaish-e-Mohammed. Modi’s government allowed a Pakistani investigation team to visit, a striking gesture given the allegations against Pakistan-based actors. The attempt at cooperation failed to produce durable trust. Pathankot became another reminder that diplomatic openings with Pakistan could be undone by armed groups operating in the shadow of the Pakistani security establishment.

Uri changed the tempo.

On 18 September 2016, militants attacked an Indian Army camp at Uri in Jammu and Kashmir. Days later, on 29 September, India announced that it had conducted surgical strikes across the Line of Control. At the official joint briefing, the Director General Military Operations said India had received credible information about terrorists positioned at launch pads along the LoC and that the army had conducted strikes to pre-empt infiltration and attacks.

The surgical strikes were militarily limited. Politically, they were expansive. The government presented them as a new threshold: India would cross the LoC to hit terrorist infrastructure and then announce it. Pakistan denied that any such strikes had taken place in the manner India described, calling the episode cross-border firing. The conflicting narratives became part of the operation’s afterlife. For India, the public announcement mattered. It told domestic audiences and adversaries that covert retaliation could become declared policy.

In 2017, the challenge came from China and Bhutan. At Doklam, near the India-Bhutan-China tri-junction, Indian and Chinese troops faced off after China attempted road construction in territory claimed by Bhutan. India intervened in support of Bhutanese interests and its own strategic concern over the Siliguri Corridor. The standoff lasted more than two months before disengagement. It showed that the Modi government was willing to confront China physically short of combat. It also showed the fragility of tri-junction politics in the Himalaya. Doklam ended without war and without final settlement.

Pulwama and Balakot raised the stakes further.

On 14 February 2019, a suicide attack in Pulwama killed forty CRPF personnel. India blamed Jaish-e-Mohammed. On 26 February, Foreign Secretary Vijay Gokhale announced that India had struck a JeM training camp at Balakot in Pakistan’s Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province. The official statement said the strike followed Pulwama, referred to JeM’s activities and leadership in Pakistan, and described the operation as intelligence-led and non-military in the sense of targeting terrorists rather than civilians or Pakistani military facilities.

Balakot crossed a line that had held since 1971: India used air power against a target inside Pakistan proper, not merely Pakistan-administered Kashmir. The next day, Pakistan launched an air operation; India protested what it called Pakistan’s violation of Indian airspace and targeting of military posts. Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman was captured after his aircraft was shot down and then returned by Pakistan. Damage assessments of Balakot became contested. India emphasised the strategic message. Pakistan emphasised denial and retaliation. The crisis ended quickly, but its meaning endured: India had demonstrated willingness to escalate vertically after a terror attack while trying to contain the escalation diplomatically.

That same year, Modi’s government transformed Jammu and Kashmir’s constitutional status. On 5 August 2019, Article 370 was effectively abrogated and the state was reorganised into two Union Territories: Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh. The government described this as constitutional integration and a path toward development and security. Critics at home and abroad described it as a unilateral downgrading of statehood, carried out amid detentions, communications restrictions, and heavy security deployment. Pakistan sought to internationalise the issue. China objected especially over Ladakh. India insisted the matter was internal.

The decision altered India’s Kashmir policy more dramatically than any step since the accession and the framing of Article 370. It did not end militancy. It did not settle India-Pakistan hostility. It did change the constitutional framework within which Kashmir would be governed. For supporters, this was the correction of a historical anomaly. For critics, it represented centralised coercion and federal diminishment. A neutral historical account must record both claims and the documented fact of sweeping security restrictions and political detentions in the immediate aftermath.

Then came Galwan.

The India-China crisis of 2020 began with tensions at multiple points along the Line of Actual Control in eastern Ladakh. On 15 June, Indian and Chinese soldiers clashed in the Galwan Valley. Twenty Indian soldiers died; China later acknowledged casualties on its side, though the numbers became politically contested. Galwan broke the emotional architecture of the 1993 and 1996 border-management regime. For decades, India and China had disagreed, patrolled, argued, and sometimes shoved, but had avoided fatalities. After Galwan, the LAC became militarised at a scale not seen in the earlier period. India moved troops, armour, artillery, air assets, and logistics into a prolonged forward posture. Talks produced disengagement at some friction points, but full de-escalation remained difficult. The crisis forced India to rethink assumptions about China: economic engagement could not guarantee border tranquillity; agreements could be strained by power shifts; infrastructure on both sides had changed the tempo of confrontation.

The Russia–Ukraine war of 2022 placed India in another test of strategic autonomy. India avoided outright condemnation of Russia in the manner sought by many Western governments, called for dialogue and diplomacy, and increased purchases of discounted Russian oil. This policy drew criticism from parts of the West but also showed India’s willingness to protect energy and defence interests while deepening ties with the United States and Europe. Modi told Vladimir Putin in September 2022 that “today’s era is not of war,” a line widely noted internationally, but India did not join sanctions. The policy was multi-alignment in practice: retain Russia, expand the West, avoid formal blocs, and protect material interests.

Relations with the United States deepened across defence, technology, intelligence, and Indo-Pacific strategy. India became more active in the Quad with the United States, Japan, and Australia. Foundational defence agreements, logistics arrangements, exercises such as Malabar, and technology initiatives all contributed to interoperability. Yet the relationship was tested by the U.S. assassination-plot case involving Gurpatwant Singh Pannun. India announced an inquiry and sought to separate the matter from the broader strategic relationship. The case brought a new kind of exposure: India’s external-security practices were being scrutinised not on a border or battlefield but in Western courts and intelligence claims.

Canada produced an even sharper diplomatic rupture. In September 2023, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau alleged a potential Indian link to the killing of Hardeep Singh Nijjar in British Columbia. India rejected the allegation as absurd and motivated. Diplomatic expulsions followed. The crisis escalated further in 2024, with Canada linking Indian diplomats to the investigation and India rejecting the allegations. These developments showed that diaspora politics, separatist activism, intelligence operations, and bilateral diplomacy had become entangled in ways earlier generations of Indian leaders had not faced at this scale.

The Maldives crisis of 2023–24 revealed another face of neighbourhood politics. President Mohamed Muizzu campaigned on an “India Out” platform and demanded the withdrawal of Indian military personnel operating aviation platforms used for medical evacuation, search-and-rescue, and surveillance. India replaced troops with civilian technical staff. The episode was a reminder that even small neighbours can impose political costs on India when domestic nationalism and great-power competition meet.

At sea, India’s role expanded. During the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden disruptions linked to Houthi attacks and piracy risks, India deployed a significant naval presence east of the Red Sea. This was not an act of formal alliance warfare. It was maritime security assertion: protect shipping, show presence, and act as a net security provider without joining every Western military framework.

Then, in 2025, the Pakistan front erupted again.

Operation Sindoor emerged from a recent India-Pakistan crisis following a deadly attack in Indian-controlled Kashmir, with India accusing Pakistan-backed militants and Pakistan denying involvement. Because official and independent after-action records remain incomplete, caution is essential. Public reporting indicates that after a deadly attack in Indian-administered Kashmir, India launched strikes against what it described as terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan and Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Pakistan denied involvement in the preceding attack and rejected Indian claims. The confrontation became one of the most serious military crises in decades between the two nuclear rivals, with misinformation and competing victory claims surrounding the episode.

Operation Sindoor, like Balakot, sits inside the Modi doctrine of punitive response below full-scale war. Its full military record will require future documentation. The historian writing too soon must resist triumphalism and scepticism alike. What is clear is the pattern: terror attack, Indian punitive strikes, Pakistani response, rapid escalation control, competing narratives, and renewed debate over deterrence.

The nation’s pulse under Modi has been intensely polarised and intensely mobilised. Supporters see a state that has restored confidence, responded to terror, built border infrastructure, elevated India globally, evacuated citizens from crises, asserted maritime reach, deepened defence partnerships, and spoken for the Global South. Critics see centralisation, majoritarian rhetoric, institutional strain, coercive Kashmir policy, pressure on dissent, and heightened risk-taking in external affairs. The neutrality demanded by history requires neither dilution nor equivalence for its own sake. It requires sourced presentation of the record and the disputes around it.

The security architecture changed visibly. The post-26/11 counterterror state continued, but Modi’s tenure added public retaliatory signalling, stronger border infrastructure, greater use of drones, satellites, cyber tools, and special operations narratives. Defence procurement included Rafale aircraft and S-400 air defence systems. Indigenous defence production and “Atmanirbhar Bharat” became official themes. The Chief of Defence Staff post was created in 2019, a reform long discussed since Kargil. Theatre-command debates continued. The armed forces faced simultaneous demands: Pakistan deterrence, China mobilisation, Indian Ocean presence, internal security support, and technology modernisation.

The economic ledger is complicated. Modi inherited an economy seeking investment and infrastructure expansion. His government promoted manufacturing, digital payments, welfare delivery, infrastructure, and production-linked incentives. Geopolitical shocks affected energy, trade, and defence. Russia–Ukraine increased discounted oil imports but exposed India to Western scrutiny. China tensions accelerated supply-chain diversification and restrictions in some technology sectors. Galwan forced large military expenditure in Ladakh. Red Sea disruptions raised shipping concerns. Defence modernisation competed with welfare and infrastructure priorities. Strategic ambition required economic stamina.

The verdict of history is not yet available because the tenure is ongoing. Provisional assessment must therefore be humble. Modi has unquestionably shifted India’s posture toward more visible deterrence, sharper public response to terrorism, deeper U.S. and Quad engagement, continued Russia balancing, expanded maritime presence, and a harder China policy after Galwan. He has also presided over unresolved crises: a militarised LAC, no final Pakistan settlement, recurrent terror-triggered escalation, strained relations with Canada, legal exposure in the United States, and neighbourhood pushback in places such as Maldives. Supporters interpret these as the costs of a rising India asserting itself. Critics interpret them as evidence of risk and over-centralisation. Both interpretations will remain in the archive.

The human behind the power is difficult to separate from the political persona. Modi’s public image is disciplined, performative, ascetic, and commanding. He speaks directly to mass audiences, uses symbolism with care, and frames national-security events within a narrative of civilisational confidence and state resolve. Unlike Manmohan Singh’s restraint or Vajpayee’s melancholy, Modi’s style seeks emotional mobilisation. That mobilisation has political power. It also raises the stakes of every crisis, because a leader who promises visible strength must decide when visibility serves strategy and when silence might serve it better.

By his third term, India stood larger in the world than at any earlier point in its independent history: economically more consequential, militarily more capable, diplomatically courted, technologically ambitious, and demographically central. It also stood at a harsher crossroads: China entrenched across a militarised frontier, Pakistan unstable yet dangerous, the United States a partner but not an ally, Russia useful but diminished, West Asia volatile, the Indian Ocean crowded, and the diaspora now part of security diplomacy in ways earlier prime ministers did not face.

The story that began with Nehru’s midnight promise had reached a country able to strike across borders, send warships into distant sea lanes, bargain with great powers, buy oil against pressure, host summits, and speak in the language of a civilisational state. But the old questions remained beneath the new vocabulary. How much force secures a nation, and how much unsettles it? How far can strategic autonomy stretch when supply chains, courts, sanctions, intelligence alliances, and diasporas bind states together? Can India become a great power without losing the constitutional argument that made it more than a state?

On the LAC, soldiers still watch the heights. On the LoC, silence is never assumed. In the Indian Ocean, ships move under uncertain skies. And in Delhi, every prime ministerial decision now carries the weight of all those who came before: Nehru’s hope, Shastri’s resolve, Indira’s command, Rajiv’s risk, Rao’s silence, Vajpayee’s paradox, Singh’s restraint, and Modi’s assertion.

The crossroads has not disappeared. It has widened.

Epilogue: The Inheritance of the Crossroads

Epilogue

The Inheritance of the Crossroads

A nation does not arrive at power in a straight line. It comes there carrying old maps, old wounds, old speeches, old silences, old graves. It comes with rivers divided by treaty, borders marked by war, refugees who became citizens, citizens who became exiles, generals who became cautionary tales, diplomats who bought time, and prime ministers who discovered that history rarely asks permission before entering the room.

India’s geopolitical posture today is not the creation of one leader, one party, one doctrine, or one war. It is an inheritance assembled across tenures, temperaments, and crises.

Nehru gave India the grammar of strategic autonomy, even before the phrase became fashionable. He believed that a newly independent civilisation-state could refuse camp politics and still matter in the world. His success was to give India a voice larger than its material power. His failure was to underestimate, most painfully in China, the degree to which moral language required military preparation behind it. The wound of 1962 never left Indian strategy. It taught later governments that sovereignty in the Himalaya was measured not only in treaties and maps, but in roads, rifles, acclimatised troops, logistics, and intelligence.

Shastri restored nerve. His decision to widen the 1965 war across the international border told Pakistan that Kashmir could not be treated as a sealed theatre of limited aggression. His tenure also joined food security to national security with rare simplicity. “Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan” was not merely slogan but diagnosis: a hungry nation cannot fight long, and an undefended nation cannot grow grain in peace.

Indira Gandhi, in her first tenure, showed what coordinated power could achieve. In 1971, diplomacy, military planning, intelligence, humanitarian crisis, and political will converged in Bangladesh’s birth. Yet the same tenure also revealed the seductions of centralised authority. The state that could act decisively abroad could turn coercive at home. The Emergency remains a warning that national strength and constitutional freedom are not automatic companions.

Morarji Desai and Charan Singh governed in the interlude between triumph and storm. Desai tried to restore moral restraint after the Emergency and resisted nuclear weaponisation while refusing nuclear subordination. Charan Singh, briefly in office, watched Afghanistan become the hinge on which U.S.–Pakistan alignment turned again. Their tenures remind us that prime ministers are not always authors of events. Sometimes they are witnesses to forces that will fall upon successors.

Indira Gandhi’s return placed India in a harsher security age: Afghanistan, Pakistan’s nuclear programme, Punjab, Sri Lanka, China talks, and Siachen. Operation Meghdoot secured the Saltoro heights and gave India lasting tactical advantage. Operation Blue Star achieved an immediate state objective but opened a wound in Sikh memory and Indian democracy. In her second tenure, command became inseparable from cost.

Rajiv Gandhi inherited fire and tried to govern as if the future could be accelerated. He reached for settlements in Punjab, Assam, and Mizoram; one failed, one remained contested, one endured. He reopened the China relationship in 1988 and showed expeditionary reach in Maldives. In Sri Lanka, however, ambition outran political control. The IPKF became a lesson repeated often in Indian strategic thinking: regional power does not automatically confer political mastery.

V. P. Singh and Chandra Shekhar governed as the old order cracked. Under V. P. Singh, Kashmir entered its insurgent phase and the Pandit exodus began, binding territory, identity, militancy, Pakistan, and human grief into one of the republic’s most painful crises. Under Chandra Shekhar, the Gulf War and balance-of-payments crisis revealed that economic weakness could threaten sovereignty as surely as military defeat. The pledging of gold was not only an economic episode. It was a warning that strategic autonomy without financial strength becomes rhetoric.

Rao understood that warning. His reforms made the economy a pillar of foreign policy. He opened Israel relations, managed the post-Soviet turn, stabilised the China border through the 1993 agreement, and preserved the nuclear option without testing. Yet Ayodhya and the 1993 Bombay blasts exposed how communal fracture could become a national-security vulnerability. Rao’s India learned that internal cohesion and external power were joined.

The coalition years of Vajpayee’s thirteen days, Deve Gowda, and Gujral did not produce grand wars, but they preserved crucial choices. Deve Gowda’s government refused the CTBT, keeping India’s nuclear option open. Gujral gave neighbourhood diplomacy a language of reassurance. Both governed under fragility, yet neither surrendered core strategic autonomy.

Vajpayee’s full premiership turned ambiguity into declaration. Pokhran-II made India an overt nuclear weapons power. Lahore sought peace under the shadow of the bomb. Kargil proved that limited war could occur between nuclear-armed neighbours. Kandahar showed the agony of hostage coercion. Parliament attack and Operation Parakram revealed both the utility and limits of coercive mobilisation. The 2003 ceasefire and 2004 Islamabad statement showed Vajpayee’s paradox: he could test, fight, mobilise, and still return to dialogue.

Manmohan Singh made economic rise and global integration central to India’s strategy. The civil nuclear agreement with the United States ended much of India’s nuclear isolation and marked a new phase in India’s relationship with Washington. After 26/11, Singh chose restraint, diplomatic pressure, and institutional reform rather than war. Whether that restraint was wisdom or missed deterrence remains disputed. What is clear is that he saw India’s rise as too valuable to be handed over to the tempo of terror.

Narendra Modi inherited the frustrations that Singh’s restraint had left unresolved. His tenure has made retaliation more visible: surgical strikes after Uri, Balakot after Pulwama, and later punitive action during the 2025 India-Pakistan crisis. On China, the Galwan clash broke the bloodless management of the LAC that had held since the mid-1970s. Disengagement arrangements have eased some friction points, but disengagement is not settlement. The LAC remains militarised, mistrustful, and central to India’s strategic future.

The India of today is more capable than the India of 1947, more economically consequential than the India of 1991, and more militarily assertive than the India of the 1950s. It is courted by the United States, necessary to the Quad, connected to Russia by defence and energy interests, watched by China, contested by Pakistan, and increasingly expected to act as a security provider in the Indian Ocean. But with weight comes exposure. The same India that can bargain with great powers must now answer questions in Western courts, manage diaspora-linked diplomatic crises, secure sea lanes, absorb energy shocks, compete in technology, and hold a long Himalayan line.

The old phrase “strategic autonomy” has survived because it keeps changing shape. Under Nehru, it meant refusal to join Cold War blocs. Under Indira Gandhi, it could include a Soviet treaty when necessity demanded. Under Rao, it meant economic opening without strategic surrender. Under Vajpayee, it meant nuclear assertion despite sanctions. Under Singh, it meant partnership with the United States without alliance. Under Modi, it means simultaneous engagement with Washington, Moscow, the Global South, West Asia, and the Indo-Pacific, even when their expectations collide.

The central question, then, is not whether India has a doctrine. It has several, layered like sediment. The deeper question is whether India can maintain the inner balance that gives external power legitimacy. Borders require soldiers. Soldiers require resources. Resources require growth. Growth requires stability. Stability requires trust in institutions. Trust requires constitutional fairness. And constitutional fairness requires that the state remember why it exists.

The republic has often learned through shock. Partition taught the cost of sovereignty. Kashmir taught the danger of unfinished accession. 1962 taught the price of unprepared idealism. 1971 taught the value of coordinated power. The Emergency taught the fragility of liberty. Siachen taught that maps can freeze men for generations. Sri Lanka taught the limits of intervention. 1991 taught that bankruptcy can discipline ideology. Kargil taught that nuclear weapons do not prevent limited war. 26/11 taught that terror could become live spectacle. Galwan taught that agreements need power behind them.

No prime minister escaped contradiction. Nehru the internationalist used force in Hyderabad and Goa. Shastri the gentle man widened war. Indira Gandhi liberated Bangladesh and imposed the Emergency. Morarji Desai rejected nuclear weapons but defended nuclear sovereignty. Rajiv Gandhi sought peace and sent troops into Sri Lanka. Rao opened the economy while Ayodhya burned. Vajpayee tested bombs and rode to Lahore. Manmohan Singh made India globally stronger while appearing domestically restrained. Modi projects decisive power while managing unresolved frontiers and complex diplomatic blowback.

This is not hypocrisy alone. It is the condition of governing India.

Bharat stands at the crossroads because it has always stood there: between civilisational memory and constitutional modernity, moral vocabulary and military necessity, autonomy and interdependence, restraint and retaliation, democracy and command. The prime ministers in these pages did not resolve that tension. They carried it, altered it, sometimes deepened it, sometimes dignified it.

At a high post in Ladakh, a soldier today looks through a night scope across terrain that Nehru’s generation mapped imperfectly and Modi’s generation has militarised heavily. In Kashmir, a family still holds keys to a home left decades ago. In Punjab, memory of 1984 has not vanished. In Dhaka, the liberation of 1971 remains both gratitude and assertion. In Colombo, the IPKF is remembered differently from Delhi. In Islamabad, India is threat, rival, obsession, and mirror. In Beijing, India is neighbour, competitor, and obstacle. In Washington, India is partner but not ally. In Moscow, India is old friend and hard bargainer.

The soul of a nation is not found only in its victories. It is found in what it refuses to forget, what it chooses to repair, and what it is willing to restrain when power is available.

India’s future will not be decided by one doctrine or one leader. It will be decided by whether the republic can join strength to judgement, memory to mercy, and ambition to institutional discipline. The crossroads is not a place India must leave behind. It is the place from which it must learn to choose, again and again, without losing itself.
